01

Units — the 100× trap

The world is centimetres. Strengths are megapascals. Between those two facts sits one conversion, and getting it wrong is wrong by exactly a hundred.

Unreal's default scale is 1 uu = 1 cm, and its physics is a cm/kg/s system throughout. So mass in kilograms and density in g/cm³ go in unconverted — a brick is 1.9 g/cm³ because that is literally what UPhysicalMaterial::Density wants.

Force is the exception. Because length is centimetres rather than metres, 1 newton = 100 Unreal force units. Gravity is -980 cm/s², so a piece's weight in force units is just mass_kg × 980 — and that number already contains the ×100. Applying it again is the classic failure.

Strengths are stored in SI megapascals so they stay checkable against Eurocode tables. A megapascal is a newton per square millimetre, so comparing a force to a strength always needs an area. That whole conversion lives in exactly one named constant, ForceUnitsPerMPaSqCm = 10000 in Core/ConnectionStrength.h (100 uu/N × 100 mm²/cm²).

That is why the conversion is a single named constant rather than a literal at each call site.
02

A force becomes compression, shear or tension

This is the idea the rest of the engine is built on. Drag the arrow. Tilt the joint. Watch which of the three numbers the same force turns into.

A joint is a plane — the face where two pieces meet — and that plane has a normal, a direction sticking out of it. A force is split against that normal, never against world axes:

  • the part along the normal is tension (pulling the faces apart) or compression (pushing them together), depending on sign;
  • whatever is left over lies in the plane, and that is shear — the faces sliding past each other.

Which means the same downward gravity is pure compression on a horizontal joint and pure shear on a vertical one. "Sideways" is the wrong mental model. Nothing about the force changed; the joint did.

Drag the arrowhead · drag the joint handle

ClassifyForce is nine lines and does exactly that: normalise the normal, dot the force into it, subtract, take what is left. The whole of the rest of the model is downstream of those nine lines.

The orientation convention, because it bites. A connection owns one normal, pointing from PieceA toward PieceB, and the force you hand it must be the force acting on PieceB. Flip the normal without also negating the force and compression silently becomes tension. That matters because mortar's tensile limit (0.1 MPa) is a hundredth of its compressive one (10 MPa) — a joint with the sign wrong gives at one percent of its real capacity, and still looks like a physics result.
03

Strength is directional — and shear depends on the squeeze

Three axes, three capacities, and the worst one decides. Then the twist: the shear capacity is not a fixed number.

Once a force is split three ways, each part is turned into a stress (force ÷ area, through that one conversion constant) and measured against its own capacity. Utilisation is stress ÷ capacity. The joint's overall utilisation is the worst of the three, because a joint fails on whichever axis runs out first. Above 1.0 it gives.

It is a ratio rather than a boolean on purpose: the same number drives the break decision and the on-screen strain readout, so what the player sees is the quantity the simulation used.

Shear follows Mohr-Coulomb

A joint's resistance to sliding is not a constant. It is:

shear capacity = cohesion + μ × compressive stress   (then clamped at a per-profile ceiling)

Cohesion is the bond itself — what the mortar glues. The second term is friction, and it grows with how hard the joint is being squeezed. Only compression contributes; a joint being pulled open gets no friction benefit at all.

And it is truncated. Friction cannot help forever — past a point the brick itself gives rather than the faces sliding — so capacity is clamped at a per-profile ceiling. Eurocode 6 puts that at 0.065 × the unit's compressive strength, which for a 20 MPa clay brick is 1.3 MPa. Without the cap, joints deep inside a tall building become effectively uncuttable, which is backwards for a demolition game: the base is exactly where cutting should work.

Squeeze the joint and watch its shear capacity

Why this earns its place, in three lines:

  • It is why masonry stands. A dry-stone wall has no bond at all. Every bit of its resistance to sliding is borrowed from the weight pressing down on it. Fixed per-axis strengths cannot express that.
  • It makes damage progressive. Cohesion is a bond — once cracked it is gone, and friction is all that is left. Real damaged masonry keeps carrying load exactly this way, which is why partly collapsed buildings stand instead of unzipping.
  • It compounds collapse. Removing support does not just raise the load on the joints above; it lowers their capacity. Two mechanisms pushing the same way is what makes a collapse progressive rather than a single snap.
Honest scope. For a low mortared wall the friction term is only a few percent of capacity — bond dominates and this barely shows. It becomes first-order in tall structures (roughly half the capacity at 10 m), in dry stone, and in any joint whose bond has already broken.
μ = 0 is not a special case. Set friction to zero and the formula collapses to three exactly independent axes — same arithmetic, no branch. That is the right answer for a bolt, which does not care how hard the pieces are pressed together, and it is what keeps connection types data rather than a second code path.

Dry stone — the one to look at

Cohesion 0. Tensile 0. Not small numbers standing in for a weak bond — exact zeroes, because there is nothing there. It carries shear purely by friction from the weight on top of it.

Squeeze it and it holds. Unweight it and it slides, with a capacity of literally zero. This is the profile Mohr-Coulomb coupling exists for; it cannot be modelled at all with fixed per-axis strengths.

Bolt — the opposite

μ = 0. A through-bolt does not care how hard the two faces are pressed together. Its shear capacity is a flat 1.0 MPa whatever sits on top.

Move the compression slider above with Bolt selected: the line is dead flat. Same formula, no branch — which is the whole point.

Source: Core/Profiles/MaterialProfiles.cpp. BondFactor is declared and deliberately unused — the connection-to-material weakest-link rule is deferred until a second, dissimilar material exists to prove it.

Density is the other units trap. g/cm³, because that is what Unreal takes. The same brick written in kg/m³ is 1900 — a thousand times heavier and still a plausible-looking number. The cross-check that pins it: 1.9 g/cm³ over a standard 215 × 102.5 × 65 mm unit (1432 cm³) is 2.72 kg, which is what a brick weighs in the hand and exactly the mass the structure tests use.
A Nail profile does not mean "a nail". Published fastener capacities are a force per fastener in kN, and this model compares stresses — it has no idea how many nails are in a joint. So nail, screw and bolt are EN 1995-1-1 characteristic capacities smeared over a reference density of one fastener per 100 cm². Every figure in those three rows scales with that assumption. When fastener count becomes a property of a joint they get re-derived from the kN figures, not re-tuned — which is why the formulae are written out in the source comments.
Lime mortar's shear cap is provably unreachable, and that is the honest outcome. Reaching 1.3 MPa of shear capacity needs (1.3 − 0.1) / 0.6 = 2.0 MPa of compression — which is exactly lime mortar's own crushing limit. So the compression axis hits 1.0 no later than shear reaches its ceiling. Lime mortar crushes rather than shearing off its cap.
05

The structure as a graph

Pieces are nodes. Connections are edges. Load flows down. Hover a brick to light up its path to the ground.

A piece is deliberately almost nothing: a mass, a grounded flag, and an identity. No actor, no transform, not even a position. That is why the load maths is plain arithmetic over a graph — fast, deterministic, and testable without a world or a ticking solver.

Load flows downward. A piece transmits its own weight plus everything it received from above into whatever connections support it. Where several supports share a piece, the load splits weighted by interface area. A grounded piece absorbs what arrives and passes nothing on — that is the earth.

Support is two-tiered, and the joint's own angle decides the tier

Take the connection's interface normal and turn it to point at the piece you are asking about. Then:

  • within 45° of vertical and pointing up at the piece → a bed joint beneath. This bears the weight.
  • within 45° of vertical and pointing down → a bed joint above. That is something resting on the piece, or the piece glued underneath something. It bears nothing. You do not rest on the thing over your head.
  • anything else → a head joint, used only as a fallback when a piece has no bed joint beneath it at all. One bed joint wins the tier outright over any number of head joints.

The 45° line is the one angle that needs no justification beyond symmetry — a joint bears when its normal is nearer vertical than horizontal. Anything else would be a tuning knob pretending to be physics.

Two more rules, both learned the hard way:

  • A support that is itself falling is not a support. It is excluded from the split entirely, not credited and ignored. Otherwise the joint that is carrying the piece under-reports by the share that evaporated — and a joint at 1.9× utilisation reading 0.95× is a joint that never breaks.
  • Order comes from the support relation, not from distance to the ground. A brick spanning a gap and the brick resting on top of it are the same distance from the earth, yet one loads the other. Routing by distance let a keystone bear none of the wall above it, and made the answer depend on the bond pattern for no physical reason.

A four-course wall in running bond · hover or tap a brick

load path to ground bed joint (bearing) head joint (idle — a bed joint wins) grounded course
Every head joint in that wall carries exactly zero, and that is correct. Each brick has bed joints beneath it, so the bed tier wins outright and the vertical joints are not in the support relation at all. They only wake up when a bed joint gives — which is section 6.
Known limitation, flagged in the design. "Substantially vertical" bakes in gravity as the only load direction. The moment explosions and kinetic impacts arrive, the routing has to generalise to the direction of the applied load rather than assuming down. The classification layer was direction-aware from day one; the routing layer was not, and routing is what decides where load ends up.
And a genuine oddity that is accepted rather than fixed. The head tier is blind to which side the joint sits on, so a piece hanging off a steeply inclined face above it is still supported by it — in tension. At 50°, a 2.72 kg brick gives compression 0, shear 2041.97, tension 1713.41 uu. That is not a bug: a brick hanging off an inclined face really is being pulled off it. It also means the 45° line looks like a cliff and is not one — just below it the same face bears nothing and the piece falls; just above it the face becomes a support that fails in tension, and the piece falls. Same answer, different route.
06

Where the graph comes from

Everything above consumes which-bricks-touch-which. Core/Layout is the code that produces it — and one line in it decides whether the whole wall is right or wrong by a factor of 41.5.

Every fixture up to this point hand-wrote its own normals and areas. A scenario places bricks; something has to decide which pairs touch, how big the shared face is, and which way the interface faces. That is two functions, split so that only one half would ever be replaced:

  • MakeInterface takes two boxes and two handles and either emits one joint or refuses. Areas, normals, orientation and validation live there and nowhere else.
  • RunningBond supplies the pairs. Being generative it emits the neighbours it knows it laid rather than searching for them — so there is no proximity tolerance in pair discovery at all — and hands each candidate to MakeInterface, which refuses the ones that turn out to be diagonals.

The inclusion runs one way: Layout.h includes Structure.h and never the reverse. FStructure stays position-free — a piece is a mass and an identity — which is exactly why the solver needs no world and the whole suite runs in about a second. Geometry lives above the solver and hands down handles.

A running-bond wall has exactly two joint areas

A brick plus a mortar joint is one cell along the wall and one course up. Running bond offsets alternate courses by half a cell, so a brick spans two bricks below it — and therefore has two bed joints, not one big one:

The geometry, closed

Every dimension here is an integer quarter of a centimetre and therefore exact in binary floating point, which is why Tests/LayoutTest.cpp compares these with no tolerance at all.

The missing strip is the cross-check that the geometry closes: the full bed face less two bed joints is 10.25 cm², which is exactly one centimetre of mortar across the brick's 10.25 cm depth — the head-joint gap below. That is asserted, not commented.

The normal is the axis of separation, and this is the whole reason the factory exists

A joint's facing direction is the axis the two boxes are separated on, signed by which handle is PieceB. It is never the direction between the two brick centres — and a running-bond wall is precisely the shape where those two answers differ.

A spanning brick sits half a cell along and one course up, so the centre-to-centre direction is diagonal: (11.25, 0, 7.5), whose normalised |Z| is 0.5547 — below cos 45° = 0.70711. Every bed joint in the wall would classify as a head joint. Gravity would resolve as shear against mortar's 0.2 MPa cohesion instead of compression against its 10 MPa. Nothing crashes. Nothing moves. The wall just stands there being wrong.

Note what does not change: the load. Both rules route the same 1333.5993125 uu into each bed joint, because the split is by area and the areas are identical. Only the classification moves — and with it, which capacity the stress is measured against. Displacement could never see this; the classification can. That is the whole argument for asserting on mechanism rather than on outcome.

A head joint cannot tell the two rules apart. For two bricks side by side in one course the centre direction is the separation axis, so both rules give exactly +X. Only the bed joint discriminates, which is why the anti-centroid assertion in the test is written against a spanning brick specifically.
The failure that matters is not a flipped normal. A consistently flipped joint reports identical loads — RoleOf turns the normal toward whichever piece it is asked about, and the accumulation signs the force by which end is loaded. What cannot be recovered from is a normal inconsistent with its A/B pairing. So MakeInterface writes the pair and the normal in the same breath, which makes the inconsistent state inexpressible rather than merely unlikely.

Why the flush end earns its place. A producer that only ever emits identical pieces is not being tested very hard. The half bat is the second piece size — 1.297521875 kg against a full brick's 2.72163125 — and its mass is derived from the box that was just laid rather than from the spec, so a half bat cannot end up weighing a full brick. (An earlier draft circulated 1.29777 kg for it. That figure does not reproduce: 10.25 × 10.25 × 6.5 = 682.90625 cm³ at 1.9 g/cm³ is 1297.521875 g.)
Refusal fails closed, and the corner is the sharp case. Two boxes form a joint when they are separated on exactly one axis by the joint thickness and overlap positively on the other two. Separated on two they meet at an edge; on three, at a point. Both are refused by one line — and a "be clearer about what we reject" rewrite that spelled it as "reject 0 or 2" would let the corner through with an area of (−1.0) × (−1.0) = +1.0 cm². Two negatives multiply into a healthy-looking positive, the normal reads +Z, and the graph gains a phantom bed joint between two bricks touching at a point — which under the two-tier rule is a real support. On refusal the out connection is zeroed, so a caller who ignored the return value gets a joint that reads as failed rather than one that reads as fine.
07

Cascade — and pulling a brick out

Solve. Break everything over capacity. Stamp it with the pass number. Re-solve so the load moves. Repeat until a pass breaks nothing. Then let the player take a brick out and do it again.

That is the whole rule. Two things about it were deliberate choices:

Every joint over capacity gives in the same pass — not the worst one, all of them. The alternative, breaking only the most overloaded joint each pass, reaches the same settled state at the cost of one full solve per joint, and in exchange it invents a sequence where there is none: three independently overloaded joints on three unconnected piers become passes 1, 2 and 3 despite nothing connecting them. A false ordering is worse than no ordering, because the visualisation will show it.

The pass number is kept. Ordering within a pass is arbitrary and nothing may depend on it. Ordering between passes is real — each break follows from the load the previous one shed — and it is the sequence a collapse is played back in. The latch on a connection records only whether a joint gave; the stamp is the only record of when. Lose the sequence and you lose the collapse: you get handed a pile of rubble instead of watching a building come down.

A joint that has given leaves the support relation entirely — out of the tier decision, out of the reachability walk, out of the load paths, exactly as if it had never been built. That is what redistribution is: the share moves onto the neighbours rather than evaporating.

The half-right version produces a plausible wrong answer. Filter broken joints out of the load path but still let them win the bearing tier, and a piece whose bed joint has gone ends up with an empty support list. It is then reported as falling, with its perfectly good head joint reading zero — self-consistent, and a wall that collapses where it should have leaned. The piece is meant to fall back onto the head joint and load it in shear.

Pulling a brick out — the MVP interaction

FStructure::RemovePiece is the player's move, and it is expressed almost entirely through the machinery already described. It marks the slot dead and severs every joint touching the piece — and because SolveLoads already drops a given joint at the very top of the tier decision, that one line takes the piece out of the tier, the reachability walk, the load paths, the accumulation order and the split at once. Removal reuses the path the cascade has always used rather than adding a second one beside it.

One line of it is not free. A grounded piece seeds the reachability walk on its own account rather than through any joint, so the walk also has to ask whether the piece is still in the structure. Without that, a removed grounded piece reports itself held up by an earth it is no longer resting on.

Removal is not failure, and the record says so. A joint that went with its brick is severed but never stamped: it never snapped, it was deleted, so it must not appear in the sequence a collapse is replayed from. That gives three states out of two accessors, with no sentinel anywhere:

StateHasGivenGetBreakPassWhat it means
intactfalseINDEX_NONEstill in the structure, still carrying
went with a piecetrueINDEX_NONEsevered by a removal — out of the graph, absent from the replay
broke in pass NtrueN ≥ 1failed under load, in that pass, in that order

And pass numbers are global to the structure, not to the call. A second cascade continues from the highest stamp already written, so a joint that gives after a brick is pulled out carries a strictly larger number than everything that gave before it. Consumers read a shared number as "these gave simultaneously", which is only true if the numbering does not restart. This was unreachable until removal existed — a second cascade on a settled structure breaks nothing — so per-call numbering survived undetected until there was a way to change the graph between calls.

…and on the wall. Pull bricks out and watch it re-route

Click a brick to remove it. Its joints leave the structure severed-but-unstamped, its handle stays valid forever, and the next solve re-routes what it was carrying onto the neighbours — or fails to, and the wall comes down.

standing no path to ground — falling stranded in a knot (section 8) removed — tombstoned joint severed with a piece
Why the wall needs tonnes on it before anything breaks. The wall above carries … against cement mortar's 10 MPa. Brick really is that strong in compression, and the model says so. The interesting failure on a small wall is not crushing, it is losing the path to the ground. That is what "it fell" means here.
A cycle is reported, not solved. Pull enough out of the bottom course and two neighbouring bricks each end up falling back onto the head joint between them — each is now the other's support. There is no rule in this design for dividing load round a loop, so the pieces caught in the knot are conservatively reported as unsupported. A course spanning a wide gap therefore reads as falling rather than arching. That is the conservative answer, not the interesting one, and someone will have to design the arching rule. Section 8 is about being able to tell, which turns out to matter more than fixing it.
08

Why a piece has no support

Four states, not a boolean. And the payoff is subtle enough to be worth a beat: one of the four is a statement about the solver, not about the structure.

IsPieceSupported answers whether, never why — and two completely different things produce the same false:

  • Real physics. Nothing is holding the piece up. Its supports are gone, or they are falling themselves.
  • A solver limitation. The piece is in an unroutable knot. Two bricks that both lose their bed joints fall back onto each other's head joints — each is now the other's support, a circle — and there is no rule here for dividing load round a loop. The solver plays safe and calls them falling.

So GetPieceSupport reports one of four:

StateMeansIs it physics?
Groundedresting on the earth; load terminates hereyes
Supportedreaches the earth through supports, and the solver could route its load thereyes
Fallingnothing is holding it up, and the solver is not the reasonyes
Strandedin an unroutable knot — ultimately one of its own supportsno — a documented limitation

Why this earns an accessor. The headline collapse test is "pull bricks until it topples, and confirm it falls at the predicted number" — and that assertion is identical in both cases. It would pass. It would stay green through a retune of every strength in the library. And it would be calibrated against the number of removals that happens to make a cycle, rather than against the number that overloads a joint. Three bricks out of the bottom course of a five-brick wall is enough to produce one, which is roughly what a player does. The test needs a precondition it could not previously express: no piece was Stranded when it went.

Nine shapes · the fixtures from Structure.PieceSupportReason

Grounded Supported Falling Stranded

Two rows in that table carry more than their share:

  • Stranded is only for pieces in the knot. A brick resting on one comes out Falling: it is not itself unroutable, it has simply lost the thing that was carrying it, and that is ordinary physics. The line is drawn by asking whether a piece's own load comes back round to it — never by asking whether the accumulation order failed to reach it. Those are not the same set: the ordering runs top-down, so a piece is unordered whenever a knot sits anywhere above it, which would strand the foundation under a knot and then everything resting on that foundation.
  • "Contains a cycle" is not "is stranded". A cycle running through a grounded piece resolves fine, because the earth absorbs what arrives and passes nothing on. A reason derived from cycle detection rather than from the solve would get that row wrong.
It is a refinement, not a second computation. GetPieceSupport calls IsPieceSupported, so IsPieceSupported(H) == (state is Grounded or Supported) holds by construction rather than by agreement. Two accessors reading one solve and telling different stories is the exact defect the stranding rule was written to close; reintroducing it one level up would be worse, because this one claims to explain.
Falling is the zero enumerator on purpose. A default-constructed or zero-filled entry then reads "nothing is holding this up", which is the direction every other accessor fails in. Grounded at zero would make an uninitialised array claim the whole structure was resting on the earth.
And there is deliberately no fifth state for "removed". IsPieceRemoved answers that immediately; this one reads solver output, which only a solve writes. A Removed value here would contradict a stale Supported from IsPieceSupported in exactly the window between a removal and the next solve — so the rule is: re-solve first. A removed piece reads the last solve's answer until something re-solves, then Falling.
09

Asking a joint its load without breaking it

Short, and it exists because of one absurdity: until recently, drawing the wall would have taken it apart.

The only evaluator there was, FConnection::ApplyForce, latches — that is the point of it. So the only way to ask a joint how loaded it was, was the same call that decides whether it snaps. A strain readout drawing joints coloured by utilisation every frame would have demolished the structure by rendering it.

There is now UtilisationUnder: the read-only half. And ApplyForce is defined as that query plus the latch rather than written beside it:

the read-only queryUtilisationUnder(F) → double
↑ the same arithmetic, once
the break decisionApplyForce(F) = UtilisationUnder(F), then latch if !(u ≤ 1.0)
↑ composed, never re-derived
the structure's accessorGetConnectionUtilisation(i) = GetConnection(i).UtilisationUnder(GetConnectionForce(i))

The single-copy discipline is the whole design, not tidiness. Two independently written versions of this arithmetic agree to a billionth forever and still differ in the last bit — and one bit of drift is enough to matter, because joints sitting at utilisation exactly 1.0 are common in the fixtures: five of them settle at exactly 1.0 and one at 1 − 1 ulp. One ulp of drift is five spurious failures in the fuzz.

Three properties fall out of the composition with no branch of their own:

  • It does not consult the latch. A joint that has already given still answers what the force would have done to it — identically to a fresh joint of the same shape. Latching stays known in exactly one place, and a caller that cares about the state asks HasGiven, which it wanted to do anyway.
  • A given joint reads zero because it carries no force, not because anything suppressed the number. Do not read a low ratio as "intact".
  • An unknown handle fails closed to double's maximum, not to zero. GetConnection hands back a placeholder whose interface area is zero, and the existing area guard answers that with Max(). Zero would read as "unloaded and perfectly healthy", which is the one answer that must never come back for something that is not a joint.

Ask any joint on the page's own cascade fixtures

The obvious "improvement" is a trap, and it has been measured. Making UtilisationUnder mirror the latch — return 0 for a joint that has given — fails exactly four assertions and nothing else: the three rows that check a given joint still answers the arithmetic, and the one that checks it matches a fresh joint of the same shape. Those four rows exist precisely to catch that edit.
A known blind spot, stated rather than glossed. The identity GetConnectionUtilisation(i) == GetConnection(i).UtilisationUnder(GetConnectionForce(i)) guards against drift, not against duplication as such: a faithful re-implementation gives identical bits and the assertion cannot see it. Replacing the delegation with an open-coded copy — even one that drops the normalise step — currently passes, because every interface normal in that fixture is already unit length. Bump the result by one ulp and eleven assertions fire, which is the margin the identity actually defends. Closing it needs a fixture joint with a non-unit normal, and that is a test change owing its own failing test first.
10

Handles that survive removal

This section used to describe a hazard. It now describes the fix — and the two things about it that are still load-bearing, because the obvious tidy-up would undo both.

Pieces live in a flat array. A connection refers to them by array position — PieceA and PieceB are plain int32 indices. And there are now three arrays kept parallel by that index:

ArrayWhat it holdsRebuilt when?
Connectionsthe joints themselvesnever — built by AddConnection
ConnectionForceswhat each one carriesfrom scratch by every solve
ConnectionBreakPasswhich pass broke itnever — grown once, never cleared

ConnectionBreakPass is never rebuilt on purpose, and it is the right call: a pass stamp cannot be recomputed from anything. Joints never heal, so a stamp once written is history, and re-running a cascade must not rewrite it.

Which is exactly what made compaction dangerous. Remove a piece from the middle of the array and compact it, and every index above that point shifts down by one — so every connection above it silently re-points at the wrong piece. No crash, no assertion. And the two output arrays fail differently: ConnectionForces would heal, because the next solve rebuilds it from scratch into something perfectly self-consistent with the wrong graph, while ConnectionBreakPass would be wrong for good. The collapse replay would show the wrong joints failing in the wrong order while GetBreakPass and HasGiven agreed perfectly with each other — they live at the same index, so they stay in lockstep no matter how badly that index lies.

What RemovePiece actually does

It tombstones the slot. The piece is marked dead, the slot stays exactly where it is, nothing ever moves down into it, and no handle ever changes meaning. Two counts come out of that, and they are different questions on purpose:

  • NumPieces() is the valid handle range, and never shrinks. Callers iterate 0..NumPieces() and resolve each handle, so made to return a live count it would silently skip real pieces in every such loop.
  • NumLivePieces() is how much is left, counted rather than cached so it cannot drift out of step with the tombstones it counts. The two diverging is the whole point.

Two things that are still true, and still load-bearing

There is no free list, and adding one would be a regression. Reusing a dead slot without a generation counter brings the bug back in a worse form: a stale handle then names a different live piece, which nothing can detect, where a dangling index can at least be range-checked. The tombstone leaks slots deliberately. Scenarios are bounded — a wall is tens to hundreds of pieces, rebuilt fresh each run — so the leak is irrelevant. If slots ever genuinely have to be reused (persistent worlds, accumulating debris), that needs generational handles — index plus a counter bumped on reuse — not a free list.
Removal is not failure, and the stamp array is where that is recorded. A joint that went with its brick is severed but never stamped. It is just as absent as one that snapped — out of the tier, out of the load paths, carrying nothing — but it must not appear in the sequence a collapse is played back from, or the replay shows joints "breaking" that were only deleted. The three states in section 7's table are what encode that, and the test asserts the property in both directions: a joint that has gone unstamped is exactly a joint touching a removed piece. So a removal cannot hide as a break, and a break cannot hide as a removal.
One smaller footgun in the same family, still open. Connections are stored undirected, but the force sign depends on which end is PieceA. Anything that ever rebuilds, sorts or deduplicates the connection array has to preserve that orientation, or compression silently becomes tension — and mortar gives at one percent of its real capacity.

What this page does not cover

Because it is not in the code yet, or because it could not be sourced from it.

  • Anything with a world in it. Everything above is world-free: no actors, no ticking solver, nothing moves. Core/Layout owns geometry but only as boxes, and FStructure itself still has no positions at all — the wall drawn on this page has them because a picture needs them, and the solver does not know they exist. The brick actor, the scenario system, the in-world visualisation and the integration tests are all still ahead.
  • The actor seam. Core/StructureBinding exists and is the first code in the project that knows an actor is a thing — one structure plus an index-parallel array of {weak actor, box, released latch}, with release defined as "not held up" and deliberately blind to Stranded versus Falling. It is still a plain struct with no world, and it is not covered here because nothing above it is built yet.
  • Fracture patterns. Wood splintering, concrete fracturing, glass shattering — designed in principle, not built. This layer decides when a joint gives, not what the pieces look like afterwards.
  • Force delivery. Explosions with radial falloff and kinetic impacts are open design threads. Everything built so far is about receiving force; nothing delivers any.
  • Connection-to-material pairing. A nail holds better in oak than in pine, and mortar bonds to porous brick but not to a sealed face. The intended rule is weakest-link rather than an N×M table, and BondFactor is reserved for it — deliberately unbuilt until a second material exists to prove it.
  • Shear direction. Shear is stored as a magnitude only. Fine for isotropic materials; it will matter once wood arrives and grain direction does.
  • Rubble that carries load. Breaking is monotonic — connections are only ever destroyed, never created at runtime. Under this model a fallen slab can never come to rest on something and start bearing weight.
