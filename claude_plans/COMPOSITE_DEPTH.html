Composite depth, and where the limit actually bites

Composite depth is how far up the wall acts as a single block instead of a stack of separate bricks. Resistance grows with the square of it — but a corbel’s overturning moment grows with the cube, and that race is the whole story.

This page has been corrected

The first version said deeper is safer and the answer flips between six and eight courses. Both were wrong. They came from the design document’s own table, which held one wall’s moment fixed while shrinking the depth beneath it — a pair no real wall presents. Building the fixture and measuring showed the opposite: deeper raking corbels are more critical, not less, and the crossover is around 36 courses. What the depth rule actually rescues is a different case entirely. Every number below is measured.

1 · What “composite” means

The same overhanging masonry, resisting the same overturning moment, two different ways. This part was right.

Today — a ladder of separate cantilevers. Each course resists on its own bed joint, only 10.25 cm deep, giving 179.48 cm³. The staircase’s bottom rung reads 22.93× capacity and the wall comes down.

Composite — one deep beam. Bonded courses resist together, so the section is the full height D: W = t·D²/6. Eleven courses give 11,627 cm³ and the same wedge reads 0.369.

2 · The race: section grows as D², moment grows as D³

Add a course to a raking corbel and you add both resistance and load — but not at the same rate.

A deeper corbel is a deeper block — but it also carries more courses, each further out, so the moment climbs as roughly k³ against a section of k². Utilisation therefore rises very nearly linearly with the number of steps, and a raking corbel is not rescued by having more wall above it.

Eleven steps is the staircase from your screenshot, at 0.369. Forty-five steps reads 1.250 and comes down. Something still breaks — which is the constraint slice 5 had to satisfy, and it does so without any depth bound at all.

3 · So what does the depth limit protect?

Not the corbel. A different case, and this one is rescued unconditionally.

A brick left with half a seat — your free-end deletion, and the jamb reveal beside an opening — reads 0.1561287 · n / m², where n is the load it carries and m the courses standing over it. Both grow together, so the ratio falls, and it never reaches 1.0 for any wall this game can build. The 7×30 free end reads 0.01588.

That is the unconditional rescue, and it is where an unbounded depth would actually hurt. Not by making corbels indestructible — they aren’t — but by letting a half seat claim the support of masonry that isn’t really bonded to it. The bound has to bite there, on how far up the block is genuinely continuous, rather than on how tall a corbel may be.

4 · The trap in the obvious implementation

Three ways to read the composite section. Only one is usable, and the tempting one-line edit is the dangerous one.

Pure bending on the composite sectioncorrect

Reproduces 0.369 for the staircase and the half-seat identity above. Floored by a min against today’s patch reading, because one course of depth gives 96.09 cm³ — smaller than the bed patch. That floor is what keeps the top-course anchors at exactly their current values.

Composite modulus and composite area

Gives 0.2476 for the staircase. Doesn’t reproduce the identity. Wrong.

Change only the section modulusindestructible

The minimal diff: swap the modulus, leave the axial term on the patch. It reads exactly zero for the staircase, and compression outgrows bending on every fixture including the 45-step corbel — so bending stops governing anywhere and nothing can ever be destroyed. The indestructible failure wearing a one-line-change costume.

Where this leaves slice 5

The mechanism is settled and the numbers are measured rather than assumed. The staircase goes from 22.93 to 0.369 and stands; your free-end deletion reads 0.01588 and stands; a 45-step raking corbel reads 1.250 and still comes down.

The design has now been wrong five times and the tests caught every one. That is the process working rather than a worry — but it is also why nothing here is asserted against a published literal any more. Every expectation is derived from the section the fixture actually presents, with the design’s figures kept only as loose cross-checks that fail in the test rather than being quietly adjusted.
