Slice 5 · the one number that decides everything

Composite depth, and why it must be capped

Composite depth is how far up the wall acts as a single block instead of a stack of separate bricks. It matters more than any other quantity in the model, because resistance grows with the square of it — and if nothing bounds it, every wall becomes indestructible.

1 · What “composite” means

The same overhanging masonry, resisting the same overturning moment, two different ways.

Today — a ladder of separate cantilevers. Each course resists on its own bed joint, and that joint is only 10.25 cm deep. Tiny section, so the bottom rung reads 22.93× capacity and the wall comes down.

Composite — one deep beam. The courses are bonded, so they resist together and the section is the full height D of bonded masonry. At 11 courses that is 82.5 cm instead of 10.25, and the same wedge reads 0.369 and stands.

2 · Resistance grows with the square of depth

Section modulus is W = t · D² / 6. Double the depth, quadruple the resistance — which is why this one choice decides the outcome.

The answer flips between six and eight courses. That is the whole point: the depth limit is not a detail to tune afterwards — it is the model. Pick it too small and nothing changes from today; pick it too large and nothing can ever be destroyed.

3 · The failure at the far end

What happens if the depth is left unbounded.

Let D run to the full wall and a 30-course wall gets 86,484 cm³ — 480× a single bed patch — putting the wedge at 0.0496. The wall becomes a monolith: one rigid block that can only fail by toppling entire, no matter how much you cut out of it. That is a worse game than the one we have now, and it is the exact risk your ruling introduced — which is why slice 5 ships with a red test proving some wall still comes down because the depth ran out.

4 · What could physically set the limit

Three candidates. The third is the one I would build, and the reason is that it needs no new data.

Span-to-depth ratio

Classic deep-beam theory: past roughly D ≈ the span, stress stops distributing further up and extra height contributes nothing. Simple and standard — but it needs the span, and a raking cut has no clean span to measure.

Bond continuity

The composite block stops where the bonding stops — at the top of the wall, at a movement joint, or at a pre-formed failure surface. A staircase cut is exactly that: a continuous path already running through bed and head joints. Physically the most honest, and the hardest to compute.

Shear transfer through the bed jointspreferred

Courses only act together if the joints between them can carry the horizontal shear composite action demands. That is the same Mohr-Coulomb capacity the model already computes — cohesion plus friction, truncated at the cap. Walk up from the cut, accumulate the shear demand, and stop at the course where the joint can no longer carry it. Self-limiting, no new material data, no tunable constant, and it makes dry stone behave correctly for free: zero cohesion means almost no composite depth, which is exactly right — you cannot corbel a dry-stone wall.

Where this leaves slice 5

The mechanism is settled and the two target numbers are known — 0.0082 for your free-end deletion and 0.369 for the staircase, both standing. What is not settled is the depth rule, and it cannot be guessed, because it is the difference between a fix and an indestructible wall.

Both those figures are idealisations that no test has confirmed. They are the target, not the assertion — the expected values get derived from whatever section the implementation actually produces, and if that disagrees with 0.369 I will tell you the number moved rather than adjust the section until it fits.
