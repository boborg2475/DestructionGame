  **THE ENTRY RULE, and it is worth more than the three tests.** *If the thing that can be wrong is arithmetic on a graph, it belongs in the fast world-free suite, which runs in milliseconds and can afford twelve thousand cases. If the thing that can be wrong is A CALL THAT NOBODY MAKES — a result computed and never pushed, a flag set and never read, a wire between two correct halves — no amount of world-free testing can reach it, and it belongs here.* The cost is a world per test, so **group by world CONFIGURATION rather than by assertion**: one test running a long sequence against one wall beats five tests paying for five worlds to say five things about it.
  - `Core/ConnectionLoad` — resolves a force into compression / tension / shear relative to a connection's interface plane, with the normal-orientation convention pinned down by the Newton's-third-law invariant.
  - `Core/ConnectionStrength` — compares a load against directional strengths in MPa and returns a utilisation ratio (>1 = the joint gives). Shear capacity follows Mohr-Coulomb, growing with compression, so a wall sheds shear resistance as the load above it is removed. Carries `ForceUnitsPerMPaSqCm`, the single SI↔Unreal conversion boundary.
  - `Core/Connection` — `FConnection`, the joint itself: two piece handles, an interface normal, an area and a strength profile, so a caller supplies only a force. Composes the two above and returns the utilisation. Giving **latches** — the breaking call reports the ratio that broke it (>1), every call after it returns 0, because a joint that has given is out of the structure and carries nothing. Phase 2's redistribution depends on that zero. **`UtilisationUnder` is the non-mutating half**, and `ApplyForce` is now *defined* as that query plus the latch rather than beside it, so exactly one copy of the arithmetic decides breaks — a second copy agrees to 1e-9 forever and still differs in the last bit, and the drift is measured in spurious cascade-fuzz failures. It is **pure arithmetic and does not consult the latch**: a given or severed joint still answers what the force would have done to it, identically to a fresh joint of the same shape, so latching stays known in one place and a caller asks `HasGiven` for the state it wanted anyway.
  - `Core/Profiles` — the shared, real-world-calibrated data. `ConnectionProfiles` carries `GeneralPurposeMortar`, `LimeMortar`, `DryStone`, `Nail`, `Screw`, `Bolt` and a `TestFixture`-classified `Unbreakable`; `MaterialProfiles` carries `StructuralConcrete` (the C30/37 calibration baseline) and `ClayBrick`, each with a density, its own directional strengths and a declared-but-unused `BondFactor`. **Every value carries a citation comment** naming the code or table it came from (EN 1992-1-1, EN 1996-1-1, BS EN 998-2, BS EN 459-1, EN 1995-1-1, EN 338). Each profile row carries an `EConnectionProfileClass`, which is what makes the physical invariants machine-checkable per kind of joint and makes an unbreakable fixture recognisable rather than merely commented. **Adding a profile is adding a row** — the well-formedness sweep in `Profiles.ConnectionInvariants` picks it up for free. The three older test files now read these instead of declaring private copies.
  - `Core/Structure` — `FStructure`, the graph that owns pieces and connections (**phases 2a and 2b: load computation, grounding, breaking, cascade and piece removal**). Support is **two-tiered** per DESIGN.md §3: a connection's interface normal, turned to point at the piece being asked about, makes it a **bed joint** beneath (substantially vertical, `|Z| > cos 45°`, pointing up at the piece — it bears), a bed joint **above** (points down — bears nothing, that is something resting on this piece), or a **head joint** (anything else). **That decision is now `FStructure::GetJointRole(ConnectionIndex, PieceIndex)`, a public method rather than a file-local `RoleOf`** *(2026-08-05)* — `SolveLoads` calls it, so a readout of a tier and the tier the load actually took are one answer instead of two that agree until they do not, and it is pure geometry needing no solve (it answers before anything has been solved and keeps answering for a joint that has **given**, which is exactly what a debugger is looking at). It was **moved, not reimplemented**: the arithmetic, the `Normalize`-rejects-NaN guard and the `!(|Z| > cos 45°)` polarity are the same lines, and the move takes one more name out of the unity-blob collision set `Structure.cpp`'s header comment records. A piece's supports are its bed joints beneath, and *only* a piece with none falls back to its head joints. Everything downstream is computed over that **support relation**, not over raw connectivity: reachability is a breadth-first walk from the grounded pieces over "who rests on me", and accumulation runs in a topological order of the same relation (Kahn — distance to the ground is *not* a valid order, since a spanning brick and the brick on it are equidistant and the second loads the first). Load splits weighted by interface area, **across only those supports that themselves reach the ground** — a share given to a falling piece never arrives, and leaves the joint actually carrying the load reporting a fraction of it. Pieces in an unroutable knot (a cycle: a course spanning a two-brick gap, each brick falling back to its neighbours' head joints) are **reported unsupported**, so `IsPieceSupported` and `GetConnectionForce` agree rather than contradicting each other silently; it is not a claim the cycle was solved. **Only the pieces caught in the knot**, identified by their own load coming back round to them (`LoadReturnsToPiece`) rather than by the Kahn pass failing to order them — the ordering runs top-down, so un-orderability is a strictly larger set that reaches down to the foundations. Pieces beneath a knot keep their support and carry everything except the unroutable contribution. Those two rules feed each other — stranding changes who reaches the ground, which changes who may take a share, so a piece resting only on the knot loses its own support — so `SolveLoads` iterates to a fixpoint, stranding at least one more piece per pass and therefore running at most `NumPieces + 1` passes. **That stranded set is now kept rather than discarded**, as `PieceStranded` beside `PieceSupported`, and `GetPieceSupport` reports the four states — `Grounded` / `Supported` / `Stranded` / `Falling`, with `Falling` deliberately the zero enumerator so a zero-filled array cannot claim the structure is resting on the earth. It is a **refinement** of `IsPieceSupported`, not a second computation: it calls it, so `IsPieceSupported(H) == (state is Grounded or Supported)` holds by construction, and `Stranded` means **in** the knot while a piece resting only on one is `Falling` (`LoadReturnsToPiece` already drew that line). Same scope as the boolean — `Falling` everywhere before a solve, the last solve's answer for a removed piece until something re-solves, no fifth `Removed` enumerator. Driven by `Structure.PieceSupportReason` (nine shapes, all four states, each row mirroring an existing fixture) and `Structure.PieceSupportDegenerateInputs`. The force handed to a joint is vertical, and **signed by which end is being held up** — `-magnitude` when the loaded piece is `PieceB`, `+magnitude` when it is `PieceA`, per `ConnectionLoad.h`'s convention that the force belongs to `PieceB`; `FConnection` then resolves it as compression on a bed joint and shear on a head joint. Solving is **non-destructive**: it never calls `ApplyForce`, so it can be re-run and nothing latches. **`GetConnectionUtilisation` is the read side of that**, and it is one line: `GetConnection(I).UtilisationUnder(GetConnectionForce(I))`, delegating rather than re-deriving so a renderer cannot become a third hand-copy of the break decision. Two answers fall out of the composition with no branch of their own — an unknown handle gets the placeholder's zero area and so fails closed to `TNumericLimits<double>::Max()` rather than to a healthy-looking zero, and a joint that has given reads 0 because it carries no **force**, not because the latch suppressed anything. `AddPiece` / `AddConnection` validate at the door and reject rather than store. **`SolveAndBreak` is the deliberate breaking step** (DESIGN.md §3, "How a structure comes apart"): solve, break *every* joint over its own capacity in the same pass, stamp each with that pass number, re-solve so the share moves onto the neighbours, repeat until a pass breaks nothing — which is the last pass and is not counted. **A joint that has given leaves the support relation entirely**, filtered once at the top of the tier decision in `SolveLoads` so it drops out of the tier, the reachability walk, the load paths and the split together; a piece whose only bed joint has gone therefore falls back to its head joints rather than being reported as falling with an intact joint reading zero. The pass stamp lives in `ConnectionBreakPass`, grown by `AddConnection` and never cleared — the latch on `FConnection` records only *whether* a joint gave, and the stamp is the only record of *when*, which is what phase 5 plays back. **Stamps are global to the structure and the numbering is not per-call**: `SolveAndBreak` starts from `max(existing stamp) + 1`, so a joint that gives after a piece is pulled out carries a strictly larger number than everything that gave before it. **Its return value is the separate, per-call question** — how many passes *this* call broke in, which is what a caller polls to learn whether its removal did anything — so it can legitimately be smaller than the numbers the same call stamped. `Structure.BreakPassesContinueAcrossCalls` pins both at once and makes them disagree deliberately (a call that returns 2 while stamping 2 and 3), because conflating them is the obvious wrong fix. This was unreachable before removal — a second cascade on a settled structure breaks nothing — so per-call numbering survived undetected until `RemovePiece` gave the graph a way to change between calls. **Two things about the break sweep are load-bearing and both were confirmed by mutation**: it holds connections **by reference** (a by-value loop latches temporaries and reports a structure that never breaks), and it **skips already-given joints** — without the skip, `HasGiven` re-reports every old break as new, so the stamp is rewritten and the cascade never terminates (the mutation hangs the suite rather than failing it). **`RemovePiece` takes a piece out of the graph**, and it is expressed almost entirely through the machinery above: it tombstones the slot (see the graph-representation section) and **severs** every joint touching the piece, so the existing `HasGiven` filter drops those joints from the tier, the walk, the load paths and the split at once, and the next solve re-routes what the piece was carrying. `FConnection::Sever` is the seam — it sets the same latch `ApplyForce` does without evaluating anything, and **`ConnectionBreakPass` is deliberately not stamped**, because a joint that went with its piece never failed under load and must not appear in the collapse sequence phase 5 replays (`HasGiven` true + `INDEX_NONE` is the "went with a piece" state, and the pair is a complete encoding with no sentinel). **Severing is not sufficient on its own**, and this is the one line of removal that is not free: a **grounded** piece seeds the reachability walk on its own account rather than through any joint, so `SolveLoads` also checks `bIsInTheStructure` when collecting the roots — without it a removed grounded piece reports itself held up by an earth it is not touching. Confirmed by mutation: dropping that one conjunct fails `RemovalDegenerateInputs`, `RemovalRedistributesLoad` and `RemovedPieceLeavesTheGraph`. **`NumSolves()` is the one observability seam on the type** *(2026-08-04)*: `SolveLoads` increments a counter at the door and nothing reads it back or branches on it. It exists because solving is deterministic and non-destructive, so solving five times to reach the state one solve describes is **invisible in every other reading of the graph** — which makes "the batched commit solves once rather than once per piece" unfalsifiable without it, and an unfalsifiable claim in a comment is not a claim. It counts **solves, not changes**: `SolveAndBreak` legitimately runs several, the fixpoint inside a single `SolveLoads` is one, and nothing here is a budget.
  - `Core/Layout` — the connection-graph **producer**, and the first code in the project that owns geometry. Everything else consumes interface areas and normals; until now every fixture hand-wrote its own. It is **two functions, split so that pair discovery is the only replaceable half**. `MakeInterface` takes two boxes and two handles and either emits one joint or refuses — areas, normals, orientation and validation live there and nowhere else. `RunningBond` supplies the pairs, because being generative it knows what it laid: neighbours along a course, and everything in the course below a piece lands on. The candidate pairs are offered to `MakeInterface`, which refuses the ones that turn out to be diagonals, so **there is exactly one place that decides whether two pieces touch**. `Layout.h` includes `Structure.h` and never the reverse — `FStructure` stays position-free, which is what keeps the solver world-free and the suite under a second. **The interface normal is the AXIS OF SEPARATION, oriented by which handle is `PieceB`, and the sign is read on that axis alone**; `CentroidB - CentroidA` is the defect the whole split exists to prevent, since a running-bond bed joint's centroid direction has `|Z| = 0.5547`, below `cos 45°`, so every bed joint in a wall would classify as a **head** joint, gravity would resolve as shear against mortar's cohesion instead of compression against its compressive strength, and nothing would crash or move — the wall would just stand there being wrong. Refusals **fail closed** by zeroing the out connection's area, routing a caller who ignored the return value through `ComputeUtilisation`'s existing area guard. `RunningBond` builds **both end treatments** — ragged (full bricks only, alternate courses one short) and flush (half bats at alternating course ends, so it is the mixed-size case) — grounds only the bottom course, and derives each piece's mass from **the box it just emitted** rather than from the spec, so a half bat cannot weigh a full brick. Both treatments give the same two joint areas, **105.0625 cm²** bed and **66.625 cm²** head; what the flush wall adds is a second piece size, the half bat at 102.5 mm and **1.297521875 kg** (10.25 x 10.25 x 6.5 = 682.90625 cm³ at 1.9 g/cm³ — an earlier draft circulated **1.29777 kg, which does not reproduce**; do not use it). **`PieceMassKg` is the one derivation of mass from geometry**, and `LayBrick` is now a caller rather than a second copy — the brick actor needs the same number for the same piece, and a second derivation is a second place for it to drift (this project has paid for that twice: the half-bat mass that circulated as 1.29777 kg and does not reproduce, and the break decision that needed `GetConnectionUtilisation` written specifically to stop a third hand-copy). Two things about it are load-bearing. **The multiplication order is part of the contract**: density first, `1.9 x 21.5 x 10.25 x 6.5 / 1000`, lands exactly on 2.72163125, while volume first lands one ulp low at 2.7216312499999997 — so a "tidy" reassociation moves every full brick in every wall. `Layout.PieceMass` asserts the exact decimal with `==` and carries a fixture precondition that the two orders still *differ*, so the day a brick format makes them agree the file says so rather than quietly losing the coverage. **And fail-closed for a mass is NaN, never zero**: `FStructure::AddPiece` deliberately *accepts* a mass of zero ("a massless piece is meaningful"), so a zero returned for a degenerate box is laundered into a real piece that weighs nothing, sits in the graph, routes load and never breaks anything — fail-*open* at the only consumer there is. NaN is refused by the `!(MassKg >= 0.0) || !IsFinite` guard `AddPiece` already has, so no second guard exists anywhere. It reuses `IsUsableBox`, the same notion of a usable box `MakeInterface` refuses on rather than a narrower one, which is also what catches the box that is inside out — **two negative extents whose signs cancel** in the product and would otherwise hand back a perfectly plausible 2.72163125 kg. Driven by `Tests/LayoutTest.cpp`: `Layout.Interface` is a 26-row accept/refuse matrix plus an explicit anti-centroid assertion, `Layout.PieceMass` is a 17-row table of hand-derived exact decimals and degenerate boxes and densities, each row asserting the *property* (`FStructure::AddPiece` accepts or refuses it, and a refusal is not a plausible mass) rather than the spelling, plus a bit-for-bit cross-check against live `LayBrick` output on a ragged and a flush wall floored at 18 pieces and two distinct masses so a half bat weighing a full brick cannot slip through, `Layout.SpanningBrick` is the smallest arrangement that carries a load (two grounded bricks, one spanning; 1333.5993125 uu each side in pure compression at 1.2693390e-4), and `Layout.RunningBond` sweeps 2 end treatments x 5 heights x 5 lengths = **50 walls and 1150 joints**, checking the emitted pairs against an **independent brute-force geometric contact scan** over the same boxes and then solving each wall. **~30 ms for all four.**
  - `Core/StructureBinding` — the seam between a piece handle and the brick the player is looking at, and the **first code that knows an actor exists**. `FStructureBinding` owns one `FStructure` plus a `TArray<FPieceBinding>` that is index-parallel to its piece array forever: an actor (**weak**, so an actor destroyed by any route reads null rather than dangling), the `FPieceBox` it was laid with, and a one-way `bReleased` latch. Still a **plain struct with no world and no `UObject`**, so it unit-tests exactly like `FStructure`; the `UWorldSubsystem` that owns one is now `World/DestructionStructureSubsystem`, below. Four decisions are load-bearing. **`GetStructure()` is `const` even on a mutable binding and there is deliberately no non-const overload** — the desync route is `Binding.GetStructure().RemovePiece(2)`, which no runtime assertion can catch because the code was never written, so a `static_assert` in the test makes `decltype` pick a non-const overload if one ever appears and **fails the build**. **There is no second is-removed flag**: `FStructure::IsPieceRemoved` is the record and the binding forwards to it, so the two cannot disagree; `RemovePiece` asks the graph first and clears the actor only if the graph says a live piece went. **The box survives removal untouched, including the removed piece's own** — a zeroed box is a well-formed zero-size box at the world origin, which reads as real geometry rather than as absent, and the box is the record of where the brick *was*, which debris, decals and replay want; the actor pointer is the only field with a lifetime problem. **Release is exactly "not held up"** — neither `Grounded` nor `Supported` — and is **blind to `Stranded` versus `Falling`**: that distinction is a diagnostic about the *solve*, and a binding that branched on it would be claiming a stranded brick should hang in the air. **But it releases only pieces the last solve actually answered for**, which is a separate condition and not a refinement of that one: `ApplyResults` asks `FStructure::HasSupportAnswer` before it acts on `GetPieceSupport`, because `Falling` is also what an *absent* answer reads as (see the polarity-inversion entry under Conventions and gotchas — this is the seam it was written for). That question is answered by **the graph, not by a field here**: `PieceSupported` is sized by `SolveLoads` and by nothing else, so its extent already *is* the set of answered handles, and a binding-side `SolvedPieceCount` would be a second copy of it — the same reason there is no second is-removed flag. It also has to be asked **per handle**: "have we ever solved?" closes the empty-array case and leaves the short-array one, which is the ordinary path (a player lays a brick on a settled wall and does not re-solve). `ApplyResults` is the **explicit push** and returns how many pieces *that call* released, which is what pins one-wayness — `IsReleased` alone cannot, since it reads true either way; `SolveLoads` releases nothing at all, because a callback from inside the solver would drag the world into the world-free layer. `ResolvePiece` fails closed on an out-of-range index, a **removed** piece (a tombstoned slot is still a valid array index, so a bounds check alone accepts it) and an unidentified binding — including against a ref that is also `INDEX_NONE`, since two unidentified things finding each other is the fail-open direction. **`AdoptLayout` is the only route from an `FBrickLayout` into a binding**, and it exists because there is no other one: `RunningBond` produces a finished `FStructure` plus a parallel `Boxes` array, the binding owns its `FStructure` privately and hands out no mutable reference, so without it every caller writes its own two-arrays-in-lockstep loop at a call site where nothing checks it — exactly the code the type exists to outlaw, moved somewhere worse. It is a **replay** — `AddPiece` per piece, `AddConnection` per joint — and it is **correct only because a layout is append-only**: `RunningBond` never removes a piece or reuses a slot, so no slot is tombstoned and handle *i* of the layout is handle *i* of the binding by construction. That premise is recorded in the code, because the moment a producer removes a piece it stops holding and every connection above the first hole names the wrong pieces. **It refuses outright, writing nothing, any layout or actor list whose arrays are already out of step** — boxes longer than pieces, pieces longer than boxes, too few actors, too many actors (the direction that looks fine), no actors, and an empty layout (which `RunningBond` refuses to produce, so accepting it would make adoption the more permissive of the two). The reasoning is worth keeping: copying index-by-index *propagates* a desync into the one type whose entire contract is that its two arrays cannot desync, and the `AddPiece`-return guard queued below would **not** close the composed case, because the mass handed over is a real one belonging to the wrong piece — or a zero, which the door accepts. Driven by `Tests/StructureBindingTest.cpp`: `RemovalCannotDangle`, `ActorLifetimeIsWeak`, `ReleaseFollowsSupport`, `ReleaseIsOneWay`, `ReleaseNeedsASolve`, `PieceRefFailsClosed`, `AdoptLayout`. **~210 ms of suite wall time for the seven**, of which `ActorLifetimeIsWeak` alone is ~110 ms against a typical ~18–23 ms — that is the one `CollectGarbage`, and it is the price of proving the weak pointer survives an actual collect rather than only a `MarkAsGarbage`.
  - `Core/PieceActions` — **the piece context menu's action table and its commit path**, and the first code in the project whose entire requirement is a shape rather than an arithmetic. `AllPieceActions()` hands back a static table of `FPieceAction` rows — a label, a `CanRun`, a `Run`, all three **free function pointers** so a row is constant-initialised and adding one allocates nothing. It is the same shape as `FNamedConnectionProfile`, deliberately: **actions are data, and adding the second and the tenth action is adding a row, never editing a switch.** One row today, `Delete`, whose `CanRun` is `!IsPieceRemoved && !IsReleased` and whose `Run` is one call to `FStructureBinding::RemovePiece`. **Both halves of that filter are needed and neither implies the other** — removed and released are separate states with separate records (a tombstone on the graph, a one-way latch on the binding), so a filter written against one passes a test that only names the other; out-of-range handles need no bounds check of their own, because `FStructure::IsPieceRemoved` already answers true for a handle naming nothing. `RunPieceAction(Binding, Ref, Action)` is the commit path and it does three things in an order where each is load-bearing. **It re-resolves the `FPieceRef` first**, which is why it is a function rather than a call to `Action.Run` at the menu's call site: the ref is the durable identity and a handle is a momentary answer, so between the click that opened the menu and the click that chose an entry the piece may have gone by another route — and refusing *before* running is what makes it a guard rather than a report (running first and then answering false leaves whatever the action did behind it, which for an action that is not `Delete` need not be visible from outside at all). **It captures the actor before the action runs**, because `GetActor` answers null for a removed piece, so an implementation that looks afterwards can never find the orphan and the brick mesh stays in the world forever with no piece naming it. **And the re-solve belongs to the commit path, not to any row** — a per-row obligation to remember it is the switch this table exists not to have, relocated, and forgetting it is the failure where the brick vanishes and the wall stands there. It is **world-free**: the orphaned actor comes back on `FPieceActionResult::ActorToDestroy` and the caller does the destroying, so this names a `UObject` and never touches a world. **`PieceActionsFor(Binding, Ref)` is the menu half**, and it takes the **ref** rather than a handle for the same reason `RunPieceAction` does: a click produces `{StructureId, PieceIndex}`, a click on the floor arrives as a *default* ref, and resolving inside means fail-closed is the same code path as "the menu for a piece that has gone". It returns **pointers into the shipped table, never copies** — the caller hands one straight to `RunPieceAction`, and a presenter comparing what it showed against what was chosen compares pointers — which the test asserts by address range rather than by label. **And `RunPieceAction` now consults `CanRun` before it runs anything.** The two guards do not overlap: the re-resolve refuses a **removed** piece, `CanRun` additionally refuses a **released** one, so leaving the commit door wider than the menu meant the rule depended on which door you came through, and every future caller — a keybind, an RPC, a replay — would silently get the permissive one. It also closed only half the window the re-resolve exists for (the re-resolve catches a piece removed between menu-open and menu-choose; `CanRun` catches one released by a cascade in the same gap), and half a closed window is worse than none because it looks closed. **If deleting falling debris is ever wanted, widen `Delete`'s `CanRun`** — one statement of the rule, in one place, that both doors read.

  **BOTH HALVES NOW TAKE A WHOLE SELECTION, AND THE SINGLE-PIECE CALLS ARE THE ONE-ELEMENT CASE OF THEM RATHER THAN A SECOND PATH** *(2026-08-04)*. `PieceActionsFor(Binding, TArrayView<const FPieceRef>)` and `RunPieceActions(Binding, Refs, Action)` are the real functions; the single-ref overloads are one-line delegations, so there is exactly one copy of the filter and exactly one copy of the guards. Four decisions are load-bearing.

  **THE MENU IS THE INTERSECTION, NOT THE UNION** — an action is offered only if *every* selected piece's `CanRun` says yes. The union is a one-word difference and produces a menu that looks perfectly reasonable while the button does less than it says; the discriminating shape on a one-row table is `{live, RELEASED}`, and it is a real case (a cascade releasing a picked brick between two clicks). **An empty selection offers nothing**, which is not vacuous truth — the empty intersection is mathematically *everything*, so a naive "nobody objected" offers the whole table against no bricks. **And one unresolvable ref empties the whole menu** rather than being skipped, which is the opposite polarity to the commit and deliberately so: an offer with a hole in it is a lie about what the button will do, whereas a commit with a hole in it has already been authorised for the pieces still there.

  **THE SOLVE IS THE LAST STATEMENT OF `RunPieceActions`, EXACTLY ONE PER CALL, UNCONDITIONALLY — INCLUDING A CALL THAT RAN NOTHING.** Exactly one is the whole point of batching (a full solve is ~30 ms at scenario scale, so five deletes done one at a time is a visible stutter for an answer one pass already had); *last* is a separate obligation from *once*, because `ApplyResults` refuses to release a piece the last solve has no answer for, so a solve placed mid-batch is still one solve and still leaves the pieces the batch orphaned hanging in the air. Unconditional because solving is non-destructive and re-runnable, so a no-op costs a solve rather than a branch — **a rule with no exceptions is one no caller can be on the wrong side of.**

  **AND THERE IS DELIBERATELY NO PUBLIC "RUN WITHOUT SOLVING".** The shared body is a file-local `RunOnePieceActionWithoutSolving`, and it is file-local for exactly that reason: a caller who forgot the solve and then pushed would release pieces against a stale answer, and **releasing is irreversible**. Same argument that put the solve in the commit path rather than in a row. An action also stays **single-piece** — no row understands sets, so adding an action is still adding a row.

  Driven by `Tests/PieceActionsTest.cpp`: `TableIsWellFormed` (a sweep, not a per-action list, modelled on `Profiles.ConnectionInvariants`), `Delete`, `CanRunFiltersTheMenu` (an 8-row matrix), `CommitRefFailsClosed` (9 ref-shapes × 2 actions, with a **test-side tripwire action counting entries into `Run`** — that counter is what distinguishes "refused before running" from "ran, then reported false", which are different bugs), `MenuOffersWhatCanRun` (12 ref-shapes, the property written over `AllPieceActions()` rather than over `Delete`), `CommitRespectsCanRun` (whose sharp row is a **second tripwire whose `CanRun` returns false but whose ref resolves perfectly**, so `CanRun` is the sole reason to refuse and the counter separates the two bugs), `MenuOffersWhatEveryPieceCanRun` (12 selection-shapes, whose expectation is *derived* over the shipped table rather than read back) and `BatchRunsEveryPieceAndSolvesOnce`. **All eight land inside the ordinary per-test frame cadence** — 17–29 ms apiece, measured as the gap between consecutive `Test Completed` lines, which is what that cadence costs anyway: none of them builds a world.

  **`BatchRunsEveryPieceAndSolvesOnce` makes the cost claim as a CONTRAST rather than an absolute** — two separate commits are asserted to cost two solves and one batch of two to cost one, both read off the same counter, so the numbers mean something even if the counter itself miscounted. Its fixture pins **ordering separately from count**: the spanner rests on *two* grounded pads and both are deleted in one batch, so a solve run mid-batch is still one solve and reports the roof `Supported` by a wall that is gone.
- `Core/PieceMenu` — **the testable half of the presenter**, and it is one function. `BuildPieceMenuRows(Actions, Ref)` turns an already-filtered menu plus the ref it is for into `FPieceMenuRow`s: a label, the action **by pointer**, and the ref. **The row carries its ref**, which is the whole reason it is a struct rather than the bare `TArray<const FPieceAction*>` the table already hands back — a presented entry is a *command* waiting to be chosen, so whoever clicks it needs both halves of the commit call, and a presenter that remembered the ref separately from the rows is one stale field away from committing the right action against the wrong brick. **It consults no `CanRun` and runs nothing**, because the filtering already happened in `PieceActionsFor` and a second copy of that policy is exactly what the table exists not to have; on today's one-row table a re-filtering presenter would be indistinguishable by output alone, which is why `Tests/PieceMenuTest.cpp` carries a **tripwire row whose `CanRun` says no**, offered anyway, plus two counters that must both stay at zero. **It fails closed on a ref missing either half** — the polarity inversion recorded under Conventions and gotchas is precisely this seam: a click on the floor arrives as a default `FPieceRef`, and a default is also what "we have no answer" looks like, so a readout may take it at face value and a *button* may not. And **a malformed row is skipped rather than dereferenced** (null row, null label), because this takes an array from anywhere and a null deref aborts the whole automation run rather than failing one test. **Zero is a real structure and a real piece**, so the sentinel is tested for by name and never by truthiness — structure 0 piece 0 is the wall the game mode builds on Play. Driven by `Presenter.PieceMenuRows`, a 12-row table whose expectation is written as the **pointers** that must come back in order, so identity and ordering are one comparison.

  **THE ROW NOW CARRIES THE WHOLE SELECTION, AND `Ref` IS DERIVED FROM IT** *(2026-08-04)*. `FPieceMenuRow::Refs` is every picked piece in pick order, for exactly the reason it carried one ref: a presented entry is a *command*, and a presenter that remembered the targets beside the rows is one stale field away from the right action on the wrong bricks. `Ref` is retained as **`Refs.Last()`** — the last-added member, the anchor a per-brick readout will hang off — and it is *derived rather than stored separately*, the same shape as `Label` being a copy of the action's own text. `Tests/PieceMultiSelectTest.cpp` asserts the two agree on every presented row, so the pair cannot drift the way two independent fields would. `BuildPieceMenuRows(Actions, TArrayView<const FPieceRef>)` is the real builder and the single-ref version delegates to it; it fails closed on an **empty** selection (a row that exists must always have a target, which is what lets everything downstream take `Refs.Last()` without a second guard) and on **any** ref missing either half, refusing the whole list rather than dropping the bad entry.

- `Core/PieceSelection` — **`FPieceSelection`, the set of bricks the player has picked**, and it is a plain struct with no world for the same reason `FStructureBinding` is one: what a selection can get wrong — a toggle that adds twice, a toggle that removes nothing, a default ref smuggled in as a brick — is arithmetic on a list. `Toggle` adds or removes and answers **"is it selected now"**, so a refused ref answers false, which is the same answer a deselection gives and is the correct one either way. **Order is insertion order and it is asserted, not merely membership**: the batched commit runs the action against these refs in this order and hands back one orphan per piece in the same order, and a `TSet` — the obvious implementation — would throw away both the order and the stable iteration a presented menu needs. **A ref missing either half is refused rather than stored**, by name and never by truthiness. **Membership is by VALUE**, which is what makes a second click a deselection: the ref that comes back has travelled through the trace, the brick actor and a menu row, so it is a different object naming the same piece — and the comparison is `FPieceRef::operator==`, written once on the ref itself so the selection, the presenter and anything after them cannot each grow their own version. `Clear` answers **whether there was anything to clear**, because "a click on empty space dismissed something" and "there was nothing up" are different events. Driven by `Core.PieceSelection.TogglingBuildsTheSet`, a 10-step **script** rather than a test per operation — a selection is a state machine and every bug worth catching is about the state left *behind* an operation — with the refusal rows deliberately sitting mid-script against a non-empty set, since against an empty one "refused" and "added then removed" are the same observation.
- `Core/PieceInspection` — **what every joint on ONE piece is doing, and it is the first time the solver's answer is computed for a human rather than for an assertion** *(2026-08-05)*. `InspectPiece(Structure, Handle)` and `InspectPiece(Binding, Ref)` hand back an `FPieceInspection`: whether the handle named a live piece at all, its support state, whether the last solve actually *answered* for it, and one `FJointInspection` per joint touching it in ascending connection order. **EVERY FIELD IS AN ACCESSOR CALL, NOT A DERIVATION** — that is the whole discipline of the type, and `PieceInspection.JointBreakout` holds it with **exact `==`** against `GetConnectionForce`, `GetConnectionUtilisation`, `GetJointRole`, `HasGiven` and `GetBreakPass`, because a re-derivation agrees to nine decimal places forever and still differs in the last bit (this project has paid for that twice — the half-bat mass and the break decision). Its worked fixture is one subject wearing **all three roles at once** plus a joint severed by a removal, so a breakout that listed only a piece's *supports* would show one row where four are due and look entirely reasonable doing it. Four decisions are load-bearing. **`ForceUu` stays in raw Unreal force units** — `ForceUnitsPerMPaSqCm` is 10,000, the newton factor times cm²→mm², so a divide-by-100 here would be a *second* open-coded conversion boundary and would put the field in different units from the accessor it is checked against; newtons are the presenter's formatting decision. **`bHasGiven` has to be a field**, because a joint that has given carries no force and so reads 0 uu at 0 utilisation — identical to an intact unloaded one, and one of those is a hole in the wall. **`bHasSupportAnswer` has to be a field** for the mirror-image reason: `EPieceSupport::Falling` is both "nothing holds this up" and "nobody has asked", so a never-solved wall would otherwise draw as a column of falling bricks. **And it lists only real connection indices**, which is what keeps `GetConnectionUtilisation`'s fail-closed `TNumericLimits<double>::Max()` off a row — the two answers a readout must never draw alike are kept apart by never showing the first and by carrying `bHasGiven` for the second. It **fails closed on anything that is not a live piece** through `IsPieceRemoved` alone (which already answers true for out-of-range, negative and `INDEX_NONE`), so a *removed* piece gets no rows and no support state — `FStructure` legitimately keeps the last solve's answer for one, which is right for a solver accessor with a documented scope and wrong beside a brick that is gone. The ref overload is `ResolvePiece` plus the handle one, with no guard of its own. Driven by `Tests/PieceInspectionTest.cpp`: `JointBreakout` (a five-piece worked fixture with hand-derived loads, five green-on-arrival fixture preconditions asking the graph directly so the arithmetic is checked even if the breakout returns nothing, a completeness sweep against a brute-force adjacency oracle, a ref-vs-handle equivalence sweep, and a two-piece cascade for the failed-under-load stamp), `RoleIsTheSolversTwoTierDecision` (an 11-spoke star whose expectation is stated as an **angle** rather than as a cosine, so agreement with the solver is evidence rather than tautology, and which asserts the **flip** from each end) and `DegenerateInputs`. **All three inside the ordinary per-test cadence; none of them builds a world.**
- **The presenter's other half is `ADestructionGamePlayerController`**, and the seam inside it is drawn at the **ray**. Turning a cursor into a world ray needs a viewport and a headless run has none; everything after that — trace, resolve, build the rows, present or dismiss — needs only a world. So `InspectAlongRay(StartCm, EndCm)` is `TracePiece` → `Find` → `PieceActionsFor` → `BuildPieceMenuRows` → `ShowPieceMenu`, and it is **a wire rather than a third opinion**: it re-derives nothing and adds no guard of its own beyond a null world/subsystem/binding. **On the controller rather than the pawn** — it owns the mapping contexts, it outlives any pawn, and it is what carries the cursor. Driven by `World.Inspect.InspectingABrickOpensItsMenu`, which inspects **per brick** over a 7-piece flush wall (a wall spawned in the right places with the wrong refs offers a perfect menu on every brick and deletes the wrong one) and then commits the row it was handed, checking that exactly the named brick went.

  **`InspectAlongRay` PRESENTS, IT DOES NOT MERELY RETURN, AND IT PRESENTS ON EVERY ROUTE INCLUDING THE MISSES.** There is one `ShowPieceMenu(Rows)` at the bottom rather than an early return per guard, so "no world", "no subsystem", "the ray hit the floor" and "the brick stands for a piece that has gone" all arrive as an empty list at the same call — and an empty list dismisses. That is what makes the miss rows able to fail at all: CURRENT_STATE used to record that they were structurally redundant, and **they are not any more**. Mutation (m3), `if (Rows.Num() > 0) { ShowPieceMenu(Rows); }` — the stateful presenter that opens on a hit and simply returns on a miss, which is the obvious shape to write — fails **exactly 2 assertions**, both of them those rows, and nothing else in the suite moves.

  **The presented state is `ShowPieceMenu` / `DismissPieceMenu` / `IsPieceMenuShown` / `GetShownPieceMenuRows`, and four things about it are load-bearing.** **`ShowPieceMenu` is DEFINED as `DismissPieceMenu()` followed by a build** — there is exactly one route out of "a menu is up", so replacing a menu, showing an empty one and closing one outright all take it, which is what makes the controls come back on all three without three copies of the restore. It is also the only defence there is against the widget leak, and **the widget now hangs off exactly that seam** — `BuildPieceMenuWidget()` sits beside the `Append` and `RemovePieceMenuWidget()` beside the `Reset` — so a second `AddViewportWidgetContent` with no matching remove, which leaks the previous menu on screen forever and which **no headless assertion can see**, cannot be written without also breaking the model-level version of the same bug (holding two menus' rows), which is asserted. The two are only the same code path while show is written this way. Mutation (m1), delete that `DismissPieceMenu()`: **13 assertion failures** across both tests, the presenter stacking up to 9 rows naming 7 different bricks. **The rows are the record and there is no second `bMenuIsUp` flag**, since an empty list dismisses; **`ShowPieceMenu` takes rows only, no separate ref**, because `FPieceMenuRow` already carries the one it commits against; and **`Rows` must not alias `ShownPieceMenuRows`**, since the dismiss empties it — no caller does that today and nothing guards it (queued below).

  **The cursor-versus-free-look conflict is settled and enforced in one place, `SetPieceMenuControls(bool)`.** While a menu is up `bShowMouseCursor` is true and `IMC_MouseLook` is removed; when it goes down both are put back, at the same named priority they were applied at. **`IMC_Default` is never removed, and that clause is the load-bearing one** — it carries `IA_InspectPiece`, which is how the player *closes* the menu by clicking elsewhere, so a presenter that cleared every context to stop the camera spinning would leave a menu nothing can close and a player who can no longer look around. It is **one function with one branch rather than two functions**, so the restore cannot fall out of step with the apply; mutation (m2), delete the restore call from `DismissPieceMenu`, fails **4 assertions** — both the explicit dismiss and the empty-list route. The controller names `MouseLookMappingContext` as its own `UPROPERTY` **and puts that same pointer into `DefaultMappingContexts`**, so there is one resolution of the path and nothing to drift. A controller with **no `ULocalPlayer`** has no Enhanced Input subsystem to remove a context from, and that fails closed — presenting still works, which `World.Inspect` asserts on a deliberately bare controller.

  **`ChoosePieceMenuRow(int32 RowIndex)` is the route from a presented row to a hole in the wall, and an index into the presented rows is its whole input** — which is what keeps the widget half to a single call, since a button knows which entry it is and nothing else. It is four steps and each one is load-bearing. **An index naming no row is REFUSED, never clamped**: `IsValidIndex` is the guard, which also makes "choose row 0 when no menu is up" the same refusal for free, because an empty array has no valid index at all. `FMath::Clamp(RowIndex, 0, Num - 1)` is the obvious wrong fix — it turns every out-of-range choice into a commit of **row 0**, a menu entry run against a brick nobody clicked — and it is measured at **48 assertion failures** (see the mutation table). **The chosen row is copied out BEFORE the dismiss**, both halves of it, because `DismissPieceMenu` `Reset()`s the very array the rows live in and a reference into `ShownPieceMenuRows` would be committed from destroyed elements; no test can assert undefined behaviour, so this one is held by the comment at the site and by this entry. **Both halves come off the ROW** rather than off anything the controller remembered — `Core/PieceMenu.h` says why the row carries its own ref, and `World.Choose` is what makes that falsifiable at all, with a two-row menu naming two different bricks and a recording tripwire as row 0 (with one row and one shipped action, "the chosen row's ref" and "the ref the menu was opened for" are the same output forever). **And it comes down first, by the one route out of "a menu is up"**, so the cursor and free-look are given back by the same restore every other route takes and the commit runs with nothing on screen naming the brick it is about to remove. The commit itself is `UDestructionStructureSubsystem::CommitPieceAction`, which re-resolves the ref, consults `CanRun`, re-solves and destroys the orphan — none of which is repeated here. Driven by `World.Choose.ChoosingARowCommitsThatRow` (the refusal matrix and the row-versus-ref property, driven directly with a hand-built menu) and `World.Choose.ChoosingDeleteTakesTheBrickOutOfTheWall` (the whole loop with nothing touched by hand: a ray in, and a re-solved wall with one brick missing out, over a 3 × 2 ragged wall whose single-brick waist is the only thing that can make the re-solve visible). **Both land inside the ordinary per-test cadence, 27 ms and 23 ms.**

  **A CLICK NOW *TOGGLES* A BRICK INTO A SELECTION, AND THE MENU IS A PROJECTION OF THAT SET RATHER THAN A SECOND RECORD OF IT** *(2026-08-04)*. `InspectAlongRay` is still the wire, and it still presents on every route including the misses — what changed is what it presents *about*. It traces, tells `SetHoveredPiece` what the ray found (a click happens at the cursor, so the same ray answers what is under it), then either toggles the hit ref into `PieceSelection` or, on a miss, **clears the whole set** — and then rebuilds the rows from the selection. There is deliberately **no branch here that shows or dismisses**: an empty selection builds no rows, and an empty row list is already how a menu comes down, so "the ray hit nothing" stays the same call as "take the menu down". **A menu is up exactly when the selection is non-empty**, which is one fact asserted in one place by `World.Select`'s `CheckMultiSelectMenu` — a menu over an empty selection is a Delete button with no target, and a selection with no menu is a player who has picked six bricks and can do nothing with them. `ChoosePieceMenuRow` copies the row's **`Refs`** out before the dismiss, clears the selection *before* committing (a brick has to still exist to be told it is no longer called out — and a picked brick carried into the next click's menu no longer resolves, at which point the intersection offers nothing at all), and commits through `CommitPieceActionForAll`, answering `RanCount > 0`.

  **HIGHLIGHT IS THREE STATES, NOT A BOOLEAN, AND THE STRONGER ONE WINS.** `EBrickHighlight` is `None` / `Hovered` / `Selected`, with `None` the zero enumerator so a zero-initialised brick is a plain one rather than one claiming to be picked — the same reason `EPieceSupport::Falling` is zero. Hover means "this is what you would hit" and selected means "this is what the menu is about"; collapse them into one flag and a brick reads as chosen the moment it is pointed at, when the one thing a player must be able to check before pressing Delete is which bricks are actually going. **One function decides — `HighlightForPiece` — and every route asks it**, which is what makes "a selected brick under the cursor stays `Selected`" true everywhere rather than at the call sites somebody remembered. Both halves are read back off `PieceSelection` and one `HoveredPiece` ref rather than remembered per brick, so there is no third record to drift; `SetHoveredPiece` refreshes the brick being *left* as well as the one being pointed at (without that, every brick the cursor has crossed stays lit and the wall ends up entirely highlighted), and it refreshes rather than clears, because the brick being left may be selected. **`HoverAlongRay` touches nothing but that**: no pick, no menu opened, none closed.

  **And the menu is finally DRAWN — Slate in C++, one button per row, and that is deliberately all it is.** `BuildPieceMenuWidget` puts an `SButton` per `ShownPieceMenuRows` entry in an `SVerticalBox` inside a centred `SBox` and hands it to `UGameViewportClient::AddViewportWidgetContent`; each button's label is the row's own `Label` and its `OnClicked` is one call to `ChoosePieceMenuRow(MyIndex)`. **No viewport means no widget, and that is the ordinary case in a test rather than an error** — a code-built world has no `UGameViewportClient` at all, so every `World.*` test runs the presenter with nothing drawn and every assertion about a menu still holds, because the rows are the record and the widget is not. **It was landed under an explicit recorded exception to the TDD gate**, not under one of CLAUDE.md's two standing exemptions; the reasoning and what remains unverified are under "The piece menu presenter" below. Slate rather than a WidgetBlueprint because a `.uasset` nobody can diff, plus a class resolved by path, is the exact hazard the required-content table exists to surface. `Slate` and `SlateCore` went into `DestructionGame.Build.cs` for it (`UMG` alone gave the headers but not the link).

- **`SetupInputComponent` binds `IA_InspectPiece`, so LMB finally reaches the chain.** `ETriggerEvent::Started`, and **exactly once**. `Triggered` fires every frame the button is held, so holding LMB would re-trace and re-present sixty times a second; `Completed` opens the menu on let-go; and a second binding runs the handler twice per click, which — now that a miss dismisses — is open-then-immediately-close. Driven by `World.Input.InspectActionReachesAHandler`, which sweeps `UEnhancedInputComponent::GetActionEventBindings()` and asserts a **count**, not "at least one"; both mutations bite for one assertion each ((m4) `Triggered` instead of `Started`, (m5) bind twice). **The handler `OnInspectPiece` is a deprojection plus one call to `InspectAlongRay` and nothing else**, because `DeprojectMousePositionToWorld` needs a viewport and is the one link in the chain no test can reach — anything put there is untested by construction, which is precisely why the presenting belongs to `InspectAlongRay` and not to the handler. It carries the only two constants in that half: `PieceMenuCursorReachCm` (10,000 cm, a reach rather than a tuned threshold — the trace either hits a brick or it does not) and `PieceMenuMappingContextPriority`.

A mouse axis is not a press: with no explicit trigger asset Enhanced Input actuates it on every frame its value is non-zero, i.e. on exactly the frames the mouse moved — which are exactly the frames on which what is under the cursor can have changed. `Started` fires on the *first* frame of a movement gesture and not again until the mouse stops and restarts, so the highlight would update once per drag and be stale for the rest of it; `Completed` fires when the mouse **stops**, so the brick called out would always be the previous one. `Started` is the obvious wrong answer precisely because it is what the binding above it uses. `OnHoverPiece` is the same deprojection-plus-one-call inch as `OnInspectPiece`, calling `HoverAlongRay`, and both handlers share the one reach constant because they are the same ray — what a click would hit and what the cursor points at must be the same brick.

  **`IA_HoverPiece` LIVES IN `IMC_Default`, NOT `IMC_MouseLook`, AND MUST NOT CONSUME ITS KEY.** The context choice is load-bearing: `SetPieceMenuControls` removes `IMC_MouseLook` for as long as a menu is up, so hover hung off it would die at exactly the moment the cursor appears and the player starts moving across bricks to pick more — the same reason `IA_InspectPiece` lives in `IMC_Default`, which is never removed. That forces it to **share `Mouse2D` with `IA_MouseLook`**, and `UInputAction::bConsumeInput` defaults to **true**: a consumed key is withheld from every mapping below it in the applied stack, so a consuming hover action takes the mouse away from free-look and **the camera silently stops turning**, with nothing logged and no test naming the cause. The asset therefore has `bConsumeInput` false, and `Content.HoverPieceIsBound` asserts it **conditionally on the sharing** rather than against a key somebody wrote down, so it keeps meaning the same thing if hover ever moves key.
- **`ADestructionGameGameMode::BeginPlay` builds the wall**, which is the only thing in the project that runs any of the chain without a test calling it by hand. One file-local `GameModeScenarioWallSpec()` — 30 across, 40 courses, flush, standard UK metric brick, general-purpose mortar — then `BuildRunningBond`, then **`SolveAndPush`**. That second call is a separate act from building and is load-bearing: a structure nobody has solved has no support answer for any piece, `EPieceSupport::Falling` is what an *absent* answer reads as, so an unsolved wall is indistinguishable from one in free fall to everything that asks. Confirmed by mutation — delete the `SolveAndPush` and all 1,220 pieces read `Falling` "as the game mode left it". A refused build needs no branch: `BuildRunningBond` spends no id on a refusal and `SolveAndPush(INDEX_NONE)` already answers zero. Driven by `World.Scenario.GameModeBuildsTheWallOnBeginPlay`.

It now subtracts the scaled local bounds centre, exactly as `BrickSpawnTransform` does, and asserts the result on **bounds**. Leave it.
  - `World/BrickActor` — `ABrickActor`, one actor per brick. Movable from spawn (a Static component can never be made to simulate, and switching mobility at release would mean recreating the body — exactly what `Release` must not do), **not ticking** (`PrimaryActorTick.bCanEverTick = false`; the solver is pushed, never polled, which is the building-scale arithmetic below), mass set **at spawn** via `SetMassOverrideInKg` and not at release, and it carries its own `FPieceRef` so there is no actor-to-handle map anywhere. **`Release` is `SetSimulatePhysics(true)` guarded by `if (Mesh->IsSimulatingPhysics()) return;`** — the physics state *is* the record, so there is no second `bReleased` here to drift from `FPieceBinding`'s. That guard is not politeness: `SetSimulatePhysics` on an already-simulating body **recreates it, and a recreated body starts from rest**, so a second `Release` on a brick a quarter-second into its fall would leave it hanging still in mid-air with nothing reporting anything wrong. The test pins that by comparing the linear velocity either side of the second call rather than by re-reading `IsSimulatingPhysics`, which a second `SetSimulatePhysics(true)` would satisfy either way. **There is no `Freeze`, no `Unrelease` and no `SetSimulating(bool)`**, and their absence is enforced at **compile** time by three `static_assert`-backed detectors in the test file — the failure mode is somebody adding one, and no runtime assertion can catch code that was never written. It also carries `SetHighlighted` / `GetHighlight`, and **`SetHighlighted` stores the state and sets an overlay material from it, and adds no decision of its own** — the argument is the state the brick should now be in rather than a nudge, so it is idempotent and order-free, and every decision about *which* state lives out in the controller where it can be asserted. **THE SEAM IS `UMeshComponent::SetOverlayMaterial`, AND SLOT 0 IS NEVER TOUCHED** *(2026-08-04)*: highlighting is additive, so `None` is a single null rather than a restore of a remembered material, and `UStaticMeshComponent` does not override `GetDefaultOverlayMaterial`, so null genuinely means "wearing nothing". The two overlays are `UPROPERTY` references to `M_BrickHover` and `M_BrickSelected`, resolved on the CDO from the `RequiredContent.h` paths — **two named properties rather than one array indexed by the enum**, because `None` wears nothing and the required-content sweep reads element 0 of a static array, where it could not tell a deliberate null from a reference that stopped resolving. Driven by `World.Brick.HighlightWearsAMaterial`, a **twelve-step transition script covering all nine ordered pairs of states** (the bug in this area is always a state left behind by the previous step) whose per-state expectation is captured on first sight rather than written down, so a `SetHighlighted` that toggled, or that only cleared coming from one particular state, fails where three one-shot checks would each pass.
  - `World/DestructionStructureSubsystem` — `UDestructionStructureSubsystem`, a `UWorldSubsystem` holding `TMap<int32, TUniquePtr<FStructureBinding>>`. **Boxed, not flat**: `Find` hands out a pointer and a caller caching one across a later `Add` would read freed storage after a `TArray` grew, so the address is stable structurally rather than by convention. `BuildRunningBond` calls `DestructionLayout::RunningBond`, spawns one `ABrickActor` per `FBrickLayout::Boxes` entry, and hands the lot to `AdoptLayout`; it **re-derives nothing** — not a position, not a joint, not a mass (the mass comes off `Layout.Structure.GetPiece(i).MassKg`, so there is not even a second *call* to `PieceMassKg` here to disagree with the graph the solver is using). The id is only spent once the structure exists, so a refused build leaves the numbering untouched. **`SolveAndPush` is the wire from the solver onto the world** — `SolveLoads`, then `ApplyResults`, then a walk releasing the `ABrickActor` of every piece `IsReleased` reports, returning `ApplyResults`' per-call count. See the world-binding section for the three things about that order and that walk which are load-bearing, and for the half that is still missing (it solves; it does not cascade).

    **`TracePiece` and `CommitPieceAction` are the world-facing ends of the click chain.** `TracePiece` turns a ray into an `FPieceHit` through five steps that each fail closed — an `ECC_Visibility` line trace (the channel the player's own click will use; a channel invented for this would be a second answer to "can you see it"), `HitResult::GetActor`, a cast to `ABrickActor`, `Find` on the id the brick carries, and `FStructureBinding::ResolvePiece` — and **both fields of the hit fail closed together**, so a caller is never handed a ref beside a handle of `INDEX_NONE` to remember to check. The ref a brick carries is treated as a *claim*, not an answer: it is resolved against the structure it names before any of it is handed back, so a brick standing in the world for a piece that has gone produces nothing. `CommitPieceAction` is `Find` → `RunPieceAction` → destroy whatever came back on `ActorToDestroy`, and **that last line is the only place the orphan can be consumed**: `RunPieceAction` is world-free by design, so without it a deleted brick's mesh stays standing in the hole it was deleted from — not merely untidy, but a collider nothing in the model knows about. There is deliberately **no second check on `bRan`** before destroying, because a commit that did nothing already hands back nothing. **`CommitPieceActionForAll(Refs, Action)` is the batched twin** and is the one `ChoosePieceMenuRow` now calls: `Find` on the structure the selection's first ref names → `RunPieceActions` (which runs them all and solves *once, last*) → destroy every orphan it handed back → **one** `PushSolvedResultsToWorld`. Looping `CommitPieceAction` instead would reach the same wall at N times the price *and* push N times, each against an answer that had seen only part of the batch. It returns `RanCount`. Driven by `Tests/PieceClickTest.cpp` (`World.Click.ClickingABrickResolvesAndCommits`), which traces **per piece** rather than once — the defect that matters is a wall spawned in the right places with the wrong refs, and only a per-brick trace can see it — and whose fixture is a 3 × 2 **ragged** wall with a single-brick waist, because the re-solve behind the commit is observable only through a piece whose support answer changes.

## Conventions and gotchas

**`StructureCascadeTest.cpp` is the one file the comment-block converter refuses, and the reason is a live compiler footgun.** The rule and the tool are in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md) ("Comment style"); every other source file was converted. Line 707 is an ASCII diagram inside a `//` comment:

```cpp
	//        [ P ]           one piece, two pads
	//        /   \
	//   lime      cement     100 cm2 each, 200 cm2 in total
```

The `\` at end of line is a **real C++ line continuation** — the compiler splices the next physical line into the same `//` comment (this is what `-Wcomment` / MSVC C4010 warn about). It is harmless here only because the spliced line happens to be another comment line. **Put a code line after a backslash-terminated `//` comment and it silently vanishes into the comment.** The converter's lexer does not model splicing, so it refuses the whole file rather than guess — the correct outcome, and the refusal is the warning.

To let the file convert: end that diagram line with something other than `\` (e.g. `//        /   \.`), then re-run `Scripts/Convert-CommentBlocks.ps1`. Not done here because it would alter comment text, which the conversion pass deliberately would not do. **Left for a human to decide** — it is a drawing, and whether the extra character spoils it is a judgement call.

**`SM_Cube`'S PIVOT IS AT A CORNER, NOT ITS CENTRE — and neither its pivot nor its 100 uu size may be assumed anywhere.** *(Measured 2026-08-03 while spawning the first wall; the test prints it on every run so it stays checkable from the log rather than from memory.)* Its local bounds are `min (0, 0, 0)` to `max (100, 100, 100)`. So placing an actor **at a box's centre puts the brick a half-size out on all three axes** — (10.75, 5.125, 3.25) cm for a full brick — while `GetActorLocation()` agrees with the layout perfectly and the wall sits visibly off its own grid. The fix is to subtract the **scaled local bounds centre** from the target centre, which is pivot-agnostic and needs no constant.

  Two consequences, and they generalise past this asset:

  - **Derive scale as `DesiredSizeCm / MeshLocalSizeCm`, from `GetBoundingBox().GetSize()`, never `/ 100.0`.** DESIGN.md §3 records "SM_Cube is authored at 100 uu, so brick scale is simply dimension ÷ 100", which is true of this placeholder and is exactly the kind of constant that is silently wrong the day a real brick mesh lands — producing a plausible-looking wall at the wrong size.
  - **Assert on `GetComponentsBoundingBox()`, never on `GetActorLocation()`.** World bounds against the layout's own box is pivot-agnostic *and* catches a 100x scale error in the same comparison, because a brick scaled by `dimension / 1` has bounds a hundred times too big while its location is perfect. A location assertion passes happily through both defects.

**A MESH THE GAME HIGHLIGHTS WITH AN OVERLAY MUST HAVE NANITE OFF, AND `SM_Cube` NOW DOES** *(turned off 2026-08-04)*. **Nanite silently ignores `OverlayMaterial`.** In 5.8 six `bOverlayMaterial = true` sites emit an overlay batch, across **four** proxies — `StaticMeshSceneProxy.cpp:1469/1611`, `SkeletalMeshSceneProxy.cpp:840`, `InstancedStaticMesh.cpp:1158`, `HierarchicalInstancedStaticMesh.cpp:1355/1487` — and **`NaniteSceneProxy.h` has none**. A Nanite-enabled mesh is given a `Nanite::FSceneProxy`, which has no overlay member at all. No gate, no fallback, no warning, no log line.

  **Get the scope of that right, because an earlier draft of this entry had it wrong in the expensive direction.** It claimed every `OverlayMaterial` reference in `Engine/Source/Runtime` sat in `StaticMeshSceneProxy.cpp` (there are ~52 files) and that only `FStaticMeshSceneProxy` emitted a batch. The **conclusion** was right and the **evidence** was wrong in the one direction that misleads: the obvious future optimisation for the 1,220-brick wall is instanced static meshes, and that wording tells whoever picks it up that ISM cannot draw overlays. **It can — ISM and HISM both do.** The real ISM hazard is a different one, recorded here rather than acted on: an overlay material without **`bUsedWithInstancedStaticMeshes`** silently falls back to the default material, which is this same bug's shape one layer along. So `ABrickActor::SetHighlighted` did everything right and the renderer dropped it, with every highlight test green. `DestructionGame.Content.BrickMeshCanDrawTheHighlightOverlay` (`Tests/BrickMeshOverlayTest.cpp`) is what holds it: it asserts **both** the authoring flag that travels in git (`NaniteSettings.bEnabled`) and the runtime reading (`HasValidNaniteData()`), because derived data can be missing on a machine that never built it and the runtime half alone would go green over an asset still authored as a Nanite mesh.

  Two things about it are worth keeping:

  - **The shared template mesh was turned off rather than the brick being given its own**, and the blast radius is **smaller than it first looked**: `Lvl_Sandbox` does **not** use `SM_Cube` at all (its meshes are `/Engine/MapTemplates/SM_Template_Map_Floor` and `/Engine/EngineSky/SM_SkySphere`), so the only consumers are `ABrickActor`'s CDO — the one `SetStaticMesh` in production, at `World/BrickActor.cpp:41` — and `BrickWorldTestSupport::SpawnFloor`, which is test-only. Deliberate either way: it is a 12-triangle prototyping cube that Nanite gains nothing on anywhere it is used, and duplicating it would drag `RequiredContent.h` and a new content path along for no benefit today. Revisit only when a real brick mesh lands. **One consequence nobody has measured**: the 1,220-brick scenario wall now renders as 1,220 classic movable primitives rather than Nanite instances, and DESIGN.md already flags full-building performance as an open risk.
  - **`Tests/BrickWorldTestSupport.h:81` spells the `SM_Cube` path a SECOND time** as `CubeMeshPath`, duplicating `DestructionContent::BrickPlaceholderMeshPath`. Pre-existing and not from this change, but it is exactly the drift `RequiredContent.h` exists to outlaw — and it is now the floor mesh whose Nanite state also changed, so the two spellings name an asset with a behavioural rule attached to it.
  - **The rule generalises past this asset**: anything the game calls out with `SetOverlayMaterial` must be a non-Nanite mesh, and a Nanite-enabled replacement re-breaks highlighting identically and silently — `ABrickActor`'s own constructor comment says a real brick mesh "changes nothing but the asset path", which is exactly the assumption that is wrong. The test is a one-row table keyed off the `ABrickActor` CDO for that reason: a second overlay-highlighted actor is a row, not a second test. **The residual**: `SetStaticMesh` is called exactly once in production, so the CDO is the whole truth *today* — a future spawn path giving a brick its own mesh (debris variants) makes the row stale silently, and the cheap hardening is a second row reading the mesh a **spawned** brick actually holds, using the world `BrickWorldTestSupport` already builds.

- **`Content.HighlightMaterialsPaintSomething` (`Tests/HighlightMaterialPaintTest.cpp`) IS THE OTHER HALF OF THE HIGHLIGHT'S CONTENT COVER, AND IT WAS GREEN ON ARRIVAL** *(2026-08-04)*. It asserts the two overlay materials would actually *draw*, which is the claim `World.Brick.HighlightWearsAMaterial` deliberately stops short of — that one proves the right **asset** is attached per state, this one proves the asset is not blank. **Four claims**, and each closes a hole the others leave: the **Unlit + Translucent pairing** as a precondition (it is what makes `EmissiveColor` and `Opacity` the two inputs that govern — switch to Default Lit and the other three assertions go on reading inputs that no longer decide the pixel, which is *worse* than failing); a **non-black emissive** and an **opacity in `(0,1]`** (a brilliant colour at zero opacity and a black at full opacity are both invisible, and neither implies the other); the two compositing to **distinguishable** looks (the property a copy-paste fix breaks, which "different assets" passes happily); and **each one differing from a BARE brick** — the row review found missing, added after.

  **That last row is the one worth understanding, because its absence was the just-fixed bug's exact shape.** The quantity simplifies to `Opacity × (Emissive − Brick)` per channel — opacity *times* contrast — which is precisely why neither the emissive assertion nor the opacity assertion implies it. Without it, dropping hover's opacity to **0.02** (a plausible slip during a look pass) leaves every other assertion passing while the highlight moves the brick by 1.6% and is invisible in game: green suite, invisible highlight. Its constant is **0.10, argued at the display** rather than as an epsilon — linear 0.18 → sRGB 0.4614 against 0.28 → 0.5658, about 26 of 255 levels — and the shipped materials clear it by 2.9× (amber) and 4.9× (cyan). Mutation-proved: the 0.02 patch fires **exactly one** assertion, which is the confirmation that nothing else was watching.

  The evaluator is an **independent oracle** — nothing in production folds material graphs, so there is no algorithm to mirror — and it **fails closed on any node class it cannot fold**, naming the class, rather than treating it as black or as visible. It does not understand material instances, textures or time-varying nodes; both assets are base materials with two constants each, and if one legitimately becomes an instance the test refuses rather than lies. **The stand-in brick is 0.18 grey and the background does NOT cancel** — it only would if the two opacities were equal, and they are 0.35 and 0.6, so the brick contributes `0.25 × B` to every channel; the verdict is unaffected (blue would need `B > 1.33` to fail) but the choice matters more now that a row measures *against* the background.

**Hard content references are a TABLE now, and there is one spelling of each path.** *(Closed 2026-08-03; what remains is the entry under the piece action table about a load helper.)* `FlyingPawn`, `PlayerController` and `BrickActor` all resolve assets by path in their constructors via `ConstructorHelpers::FObjectFinder`, which fails at construction rather than at compile time — so deleting an asset used to leave a null pointer and nothing saying so. `Source/DestructionGame/RequiredContent.h` now names each path as a constant, `RequiredContentPaths()` returns the table of them, and **the three constructors take their paths from those constants**, so the table cannot become a parallel list of paths that all happen to load while the game resolves something else.

  `DestructionGame.Content.RequiredAssetsResolve` is what makes it a red test, and it makes **three** claims that close different holes: every path in the table loads through `StaticLoadObject`; no CDO is holding a null, read **by reflection** off each class's own declared properties (plus the brick mesh by hand, since it is set onto a component rather than being a `UPROPERTY`); and every asset a CDO actually resolved appears in the table **by its own `GetPathName()`**. Adding a hard reference is adding a row — and forgetting the row is a failure, which is the direction that matters.

**`NewObject<UObject>` DOES NOT WORK, and it fails as a test ERROR rather than as a compile error.** *(Hit 2026-08-02 writing `Tests/StructureBindingTest.cpp`, which needs a throwaway `UObject` to stand in for a brick actor.)* `UObject` itself is flagged abstract, so instantiating it directly trips an engine ensure — *"Class which was marked abstract was trying to be loaded in Outer /Engine/Transient"* — and the automation framework counts that ensure as a failure of whatever test was running. Use a **concrete** type: `NewObject<UStaticMeshComponent>(GetTransientPackage())` is what the binding tests use, it needs no world, and it is the nearest concrete thing a brick will genuinely own. Note a transient-package `UObject` is enough for anything that only holds, hands back and outlives a pointer — spinning up a world to get a real `AActor` would test the next slice by accident.

**`TArray::Add` ASSERTS when its argument aliases the array's own storage, and it kills the whole automation run.** *(Hit 2026-08-03 writing the `AdoptLayout` desync fixture.)* `Extra.Boxes.Add(Extra.Boxes[0])` is a check failure inside `TArray`, not a test failure — the process aborts, so the tests after it never run and the log carries no `Test Completed` line for any of them. `Add` may reallocate, at which point the reference it was handed points into freed storage; the check is there deliberately. **Copy out to a local first** (`const FPieceBox Duplicate = Extra.Boxes[0]; Extra.Boxes.Add(Duplicate);`). Same applies to `Emplace`, `Insert` and `Push`.

**`RunningBond`'s real desync route CRASHES the run rather than failing a test, so build the desynced STATE by hand instead.** *(Observed 2026-08-03 writing the same fixture.)* The route is recorded under the layout producer: an infinite `BrickSizeCm.X` passes the `!(x > 0.0)` spec guard, `PieceMassKg` answers with something `AddPiece` refuses, `LayBrick` appends the box anyway and returns `INDEX_NONE` as though it were a handle. That is the desync — but the *same* input then drives `JoinIfTouching` into `Layout.Boxes[INDEX_NONE]`, which aborts the process with **exit 3** and takes the rest of the suite with it. A test that wants a desynced layout should therefore append a copied box, or add a spare piece, to a wall that laid correctly. The state is what the assertion is about; the route there is not.

**A `TestTrue(FString::Printf(..., SideEffect()), Condition)` IS UNSEQUENCED, AND MSVC EVALUATES THE CONDITION FIRST.** *(Hit 2026-08-03 writing `Core.PieceActions.MenuOffersWhatCanRun`'s fixture.)* The two arguments to a call may be evaluated in either order, so folding a state-changing call into the message of the assertion it sets up — `TestTrue(FString::Printf(TEXT("...%d"), Binding.ApplyResults()), Binding.IsReleased(2))` — read the flag **before** the call that sets it, and the fixture went red for a reason that had nothing to do with the code under test. **Make the side effect its own statement**, capture the result in a local, and use the local in both. The comment in that fixture records the trap at the site.

**Automation runs exit 0 even when tests fail**, and results never reach stdout. Always read `Saved/Logs/DestructionGame.log`. See [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md).

  **Every run also reports `6382 tests available`** — that is the engine-wide automation list, and the project filter is the only thing keeping a run small. A typo or a wrong filter silently runs far more or far fewer tests than intended, and **the count of `Test Completed` lines is the only guard**. Check that count against what you expected; it is the same reason the exit code is worthless.

**A functional test is a map edit, not just a new file.** Discovery goes through the asset-registry tag `TestNames`, written onto the `.umap` by `FFunctionalTestingModule::OnGetAssetTagsForWorld` (`Engine/Source/Developer/FunctionalTesting/Private/FunctionalTestingModule.cpp:65-102`) **when the level is saved in the editor**; `GetMapTests` (same file, `:115`) reads it back off disk with `bIncludeOnlyOnDiskAssets = true`. The C++ class alone registers nothing — the actor must be placed in a map and the map re-saved. So every world-based test is a new file **plus an editor session**: binary asset diffs, merge conflicts on the map, and a test that cannot be read in a pull request. **CLAUDE.md's "a new test is just a new file" holds only for the arithmetic tests** — see Housekeeping for the doc edits that owes.

**Functional tests register under a different path prefix, and the wrong filter fails silently.** They land at `Project.Functional Tests.<map path>.<actor label>`, fixed by `MapPackageToAutomationPath` (`FunctionalTest.cpp:131`), *not* under `DestructionGame.*` — so the documented `Automation RunTests DestructionGame` **does not run them and says nothing about it** (a baseline run with that filter reported exactly the 28 arithmetic tests and no functional one). That is the dangerous direction: a wrong filter produces a green run of the wrong set. The `+` separator works and tolerates the space in the suite name:

```
-ExecCmds="Automation RunTests DestructionGame+Project.Functional Tests"
```

  There is a `TestPathOverride` asset-tag hook that would rename the prefix, but the engine never writes that tag, so using it means shipping our own `GetAssetTagsWithContext` provider — production code, which needs its own red test first.

**Functional-test maps are cook-gated, and the gate is two halves that only work together.** *(Set up 2026-08-01, while there were still zero functional tests — deliberately, because once tests are scattered through maps this becomes a migration with a binary `.umap` diff per test.)* The rule itself is in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md): `AFunctionalTest` and every subclass may be placed **only** in maps under `Content/Maps/FunctionalTests/`. `Config/DefaultGame.ini` supplies the other half, `+DirectoriesToNeverCook=(Path="/Game/Maps/FunctionalTests")`. Neither half is sufficient alone — the ini excludes a folder, and only the placement rule guarantees every test actor is inside it.

  Why: `AFunctionalTest` is an actor serialised into a `.umap`, and its class lives in Epic's `FunctionalTesting` module, which `Engine/Source/Developer/FunctionalTesting/FunctionalTesting.Build.cs` precompiles only when `Target.Configuration != Shipping`. A cooked map holding a placed test actor therefore carries a **dangling class reference** into a Shipping build. `WITH_DEV_AUTOMATION_TESTS` cannot help, because the actor has to be a real `UCLASS` in a saved map. The point of the folder rule is to make the situation **structurally impossible** rather than something to test for.

  Four things worth not re-deriving:

  - **The ini section is `[/Script/UnrealEd.ProjectPackagingSettings]` even though the class moved to the `DeveloperToolSettings` module in UE5.** `UProjectPackagingSettings::OverrideConfigSection` (`ProjectPackagingSettings.h:190-193`) pins the legacy name for back-compat, and `BaseEngine.ini` carries the matching `ClassRedirects` entry. "Correcting" the header to `DeveloperToolSettings` makes the whole section **silently ignored** — no warning, no error, and the gate is simply gone.
  - **`DirectoriesToNeverCook` is a long package path, not a filesystem path.** `UCookOnTheFlyServer::GetNeverCookPackageNames` (`CookOnTheFlyServer.cpp:12438-12463`) feeds each entry to `FPackageName::TryConvertGameRelativePackagePathToLocalPath`, and an entry that fails to convert is logged only at `Verbose` and otherwise ignored. It is also applied **only** under `IsDirectorCookByTheBook()`, which is the real packaging cook — so it does nothing to cook-on-the-fly, which is correct and not a gap.
  - **There is no `MapsToCook` allowlist and none was added.** Confirmed empty by dumping the CDO. An allowlist would be the other way to achieve this (simply never list a test map), but it restricts what gets cooked project-wide when no map list is given on the commandline — a much broader behavioural change to packaging, with real scope to break a future package for reasons unrelated to tests. The deny-list is the targeted mechanism and was chosen for that reason.
  - **`Build.cs` was deliberately left untouched.** `PrivateDependencyModuleNames.Add("FunctionalTesting")` is the whole dependency (harness spike), but nothing needs it until the first functional test exists, and an unused dependency is speculative. When it is added it must be **gated** — `if (Target.Configuration != UnrealTargetConfiguration.Shipping) { ... }` — and that form is recorded in CLAUDE.md precisely so nobody adds it bare.

  **What is verified and what is not.** Verified: the ini parses and reaches the settings object — a headless `obj dump ProjectPackagingSettings` reports `DirectoriesToNeverCook(0)=(Path="/Game/Maps/FunctionalTests")` on the CDO, which rules out the silent-ignore failure mode above; and the editor build plus all 28 tests are unaffected. **Not verified: that a Shipping package actually succeeds or that the exclusion takes effect in a real cook.** No Shipping build has ever been attempted on this project and none was attempted here — it is slow, there is no shipping configuration set up, and a failure would most likely be unrelated noise. The first real package is still where that gets proven.

  One related knob left alone: `TestDirectoriesToNotSearch` is also empty, and the cooker adds it to the same never-cook list. It is about editor-time enumeration of packages that error on load, which is not our problem, and setting it would be redundant for cooking.

**An anonymous namespace is private to a TRANSLATION UNIT, not to a file — and this applies to every `.cpp` in the module, production as much as tests.** A unity build merges many files into one TU, at which point every anonymous namespace in the blob is the *same* namespace. Two file-local names that collide are then a hard compile error, and it does not matter that the files never refer to each other.

  **THE LANDMINE IS ARMED. It armed itself somewhere between 17 and 26 source files, and the earlier text here — "it compiles non-unity regardless of git status, `-StressTestUnity`, or what is committed" — is now FALSE and was corrected 2026-08-03 after it went off.** The module has 26 `.cpp` files today, and an ordinary `Build.bat` run compiles `Module.DestructionGame.1.cpp`: a real unity blob. Adding the three `World/` files was enough to change the partitioning and break the build immediately, with `error C2872: 'BedJointCosine': ambiguous symbol` between `Structure.cpp`'s anonymous namespace and `LayoutTestSupport`.

  **Adaptive unity is what decides who is in the blob, and it keys off `git status`** — the log line *"Using 'git status' to determine working set"* really is that, and the `[Adaptive Build] Excluded from DestructionGame unity file: …` line names the files left out. So **a file you are editing is outside the blob and a file you committed is inside it**, which means the same source collides or does not depending on what happens to be dirty. That is the worst possible property for a build failure: it appears when you commit and disappears when you touch the file again.

**`ConnectionStrength.ShearCap`'s compressions are literals chosen against mortar's current numbers.** The test feeds 3.0 and 4.0 MPa of compression, which have to sit in a window to measure what the test is named for: **above** where the cap bites (`(MaxShear - Cohesion) / mu`, currently `(1.3 - 0.2) / 0.6` = 1.83 MPa — below it, friction is uncapped and the test measures plain Mohr-Coulomb) and **far enough below** mortar's compressive limit that shear still governs (at 4.0 MPa compression is at 0.4 against shear's 0.77). The ceiling itself is read from the profile, so it tracks a retune; these two inputs do not. Low risk — the assertions fail loudly rather than silently if the window moves — but a retune that raised the cap past ~2.6 MPa or dropped compressive strength toward 5 MPa would quietly change what is under test. Fix by deriving the compressions from the bite point, with a fixture precondition asserting the window, as `Profiles.DryStoneCarriesLoadByFrictionAlone` does.

**An invariant asserted over fixtures that all share a hidden property is not an invariant.** "A gravity load path never pulls a joint open" was green across the whole suite — because every interface normal in every fixture was axis-aligned. Whoever next touches the door guard should mutate it — delete the `Actors.Num() != PieceCount` clause, say — rather than trusting them.

**Reverting a mutation: `git checkout` only if the file is COMMITTED. Otherwise copy — and touch it afterwards.** This process depends on mutate-then-revert, and both ways of reverting have a trap. Read both before choosing.

- **UBT decides what to rebuild by timestamp.** Restoring a file with its original mtime — `Copy-Item`, `robocopy`, anything preserving timestamps — leaves UBT considering the `.obj` up to date, so the "reverted" build silently keeps running the **mutation** and produces a green-looking summary from the wrong binary. Caught once only because the fuzz's joint counts looked wrong. If you copy a file back, **touch it** and confirm the link step actually ran.
- **`git checkout -- <path>` reverts to HEAD, not to your working state.** It stamps a fresh mtime, so it solves the problem above — but if the file has **uncommitted work in it, that work is destroyed**, with no stash, no dangling blob and nothing in the reflog to recover from. This happened: a mutation revert during the removal slice wiped `RemovePiece`, `IsPieceRemoved`, `NumLivePieces`, `AddPiece`'s flag and the `SolveLoads` root guard, none of which were committed. They were rebuilt from context and the suite returned to exactly its prior state, but some comment prose is a reconstruction rather than the original.

**So: before mutating an uncommitted file, copy it somewhere first, and revert by copying back plus a touch.** `git checkout` is the right tool only when HEAD already holds what you want back. An earlier version of this entry recommended `git checkout` unconditionally, which is what caused the loss.

**1 newton = 100 Unreal force units.** World scale is 1 uu = 1 cm, so force and impulse need a ×100 conversion while mass (kg) and density (g/cm³) need none. Strengths are stored in SI and converted at one named boundary. Full table in [DESIGN.md §3](DESIGN.md).

**The NaN guards and the shear cap are coupled — don't remove one.** `FMath::Min` is `(A <= B) ? A : B`, so `Min(NaN, cap)` returns the **cap**: a NaN shear capacity silently becomes a plausible number. The result still fails closed only because the compression axis is separately guarded by `IsFinite` and returns `Max()`, which then dominates. Drop that guard and the cap will launder NaN into a believable value.

  The related edge — a cap set *below* cohesion silently giving a joint less strength than its stated cohesion — is no longer untested at the **data** level: `Profiles.ConnectionInvariants` and `Profiles.MaterialInvariants` both sweep every row and reject a `MaxShearStrengthMPa` under its `ShearCohesionMPa`, so no profile in the library can be built that way. `ComputeUtilisation` itself still has no opinion on it, which is the right division of labour but worth knowing if a strength is ever constructed outside the library.

  **Dependents of that never-NaN guarantee**, so a refactor can find them: the shear cap in `ComputeUtilisation` itself, and the break comparison in `FConnection::ApplyForce` — the one line that decides whether a joint gives. The latter is written `!(x <= 1.0)` so it is locally correct even if the guarantee were ever broken; don't "simplify" it back to `x > 1.0`.

**`ComputeUtilisation`'s "always finite for any input" is slightly overstated.** A subnormal-but-positive capacity (e.g. `5e-324`) passes the `> 0.0` guard and yields `+Inf`. Unreachable from any real profile, and the break decision stays correct since `Inf > 1.0` — but a caller *summing* utilisations for a strain readout would get infinity. Tighten the wording or the guard when the readouts land.

**Degenerate inputs fail closed, and `FMath::Max` is why it matters.** `Max` is `(A >= B) ? A : B`, so every comparison against NaN is false and `Max3` *silently discards* a NaN — returning whichever other axis was lowest. A NaN load therefore produced a confident utilisation of **0.0**, meaning "unstressed, perfectly fine", which nothing downstream could detect. `ComputeUtilisation` now returns `TNumericLimits<double>::Max()` for a non-positive interface area or any non-finite stress. Guards are written `!(x > 0.0)` rather than `x <= 0.0` so a NaN is caught by the same branch instead of slipping past it. Locked down by `ConnectionStrength.DegenerateInputs`.

**A value that is safe as an ANSWER is unsafe as a COMMAND — fail-closed inverts polarity at every seam where a diagnostic becomes an action.** *(Found 2026-08-02 in `FStructureBinding::ApplyResults`; the guard is in, and the general trap is not.)* `EPieceSupport::Falling` sits at enumerator zero **deliberately**, so an absent or zero-filled solver answer reads "nothing is holding this up" rather than claiming the structure rests on the earth. That is fail-**closed** for a *diagnostic*: it promises least. One layer up, `Falling` is the trigger for handing a brick to physics and latching it forever — and the same default is then fail-**open**. Before any solve `FStructure::PieceSupported` is empty, so every handle reads `Falling`, so `ApplyResults` released the entire structure, grounded foundation included, permanently. Nothing crashed and no later solve could undo it.

  The lesson generalises past this one call site, and is the same shape as the caller obligation two entries down: **a readout may take a fail-closed default at face value; anything irreversible must first ask whether there is an answer at all.** The house pattern for asking is `FStructureBinding::RemovePiece` — consult the graph and act only if the graph said yes — and the accessor added for it is `FStructure::HasSupportAnswer`, which reports the last solve's own extent rather than a "have we solved" flag. **Look for this trap wherever an `EPieceSupport`, an `IsPieceSupported` or a utilisation crosses out of the world-free layer**: a `SolveAndBreak` on the binding, the brick actor, the release path in any scenario. Two ways to be past the extent and a guard can close one without closing the other — the array being **empty** (nothing solved) and the array being **short** (a piece added since) — so `DestructionGame.Core.StructureBinding.ReleaseNeedsASolve` has a row for each, and both were confirmed to fail alone by mutation (asking `HasSupportAnswer(0)` instead of `(PieceIndex)` fails only row 2; gating the guard on a non-empty extent fails only row 1).

**A degenerate interface normal only fails closed inside `FConnection`.** `ClassifyForce` answers a zero-length or NaN normal with a *zero load*, which is right in isolation but reads downstream as "unloaded, perfectly healthy" — the fail-open hole appears only once the two halves are composed. `FConnection::ApplyForce` closes it by substituting a zero interface area, routing the case through `ComputeUtilisation`'s existing area guard rather than adding a second one. **Anything else that calls `ClassifyForce` and `ComputeUtilisation` directly re-opens the hole** and must make the same check (`FVector::Normalize()` returns false for both the zero-length and the NaN case, so one test covers both). Locked down by the bad-normal rows of `Connection.DegenerateInputs`.

## What a "piece" is at building scale *(decided 2026-08-03)*

**One actor per brick, and a brick is not the unit at building scale.** The MVP builds `ABrickActor` per piece — twenty bricks, it is free, and it keeps handle stability, which this project has already paid for once (`UInstancedStaticMeshComponent::RemoveInstance` shifts every higher index down, which is exactly the renumbering the tombstone scheme exists to outlaw, reintroduced in a second array the solver cannot see).

The reason that is safe is that **individual bricks are never the unit for a large building.** Worked through: a 20-storey building, 30 m square, 3.5 m floors, is 70 m tall with a 120 m perimeter, so ~8,400 m² of facade, ~5,000 m² solid after glazing. A brick face plus joints is 225 x 75 mm = 0.0169 m², so ~59 bricks/m² — **≈295,000 bricks single leaf**, twice that for a cavity wall. (Sanity check: a 3-bed house is 8–10,000 bricks over ~150 m², the same ~60/m².) But a 20-storey building is **not held up by its bricks** — it is a frame with brick hung on it, so the load-bearing pieces are columns, beams and slabs: roughly **3,000–5,000**.

So the answer is not instancing 300,000 individually-simulated bricks. It is that **a wall is a few large panels while it is intact, and a panel becomes bricks when something hits it** — which is what the deferred *piece-size floor + three modes* item is for, and exactly what geometry collections are the right tool for (one piece fracturing into fragments; see DESIGN.md §3). That gives 300,000 bricks in principle without 300,000 pieces at once.

**Consequences to hold on to:** `ABrickActor` sets `PrimaryActorTick.bCanEverTick = false` — done, and asserted per brick by `World.Brick.SpawnedWallMatchesItsLayout` — because the solver is pushed, never polled, and a per-brick tick is what makes thousands of actors expensive rather than merely numerous. And the swap point for any future representation change is already structural: `bReleased` is one-way and `ApplyResults` is an explicit push, so intact pieces could become instanced later while released ones stay real dynamic bodies, with no change to the actor class, the `FPieceRef`, or the release call.

## MVP: a wall you can watch break

The agreed next milestone. **Two bricks and one joint first** — visible, breakable, exercising the whole pipeline end to end — then scale to a wall, so later problems are wall logic rather than plumbing.

Architecture settled by spike (DESIGN.md §3): pieces are **kinematic while intact**, we compute connection loads ourselves, and a piece switches to dynamic when the connection holding it gives. **No Geometry Collections** — those are for pieces fracturing into smaller pieces, which the MVP does not need. ### The scenario wall, MEASURED *(2026-08-04)*

A flush 30 × 40 running bond: **1,220 pieces, 3,520 joints**, spanning X −10.75..663.25 and reaching Z 299 — 6.74 m long, just under 3 m tall. Reported (never asserted — a wall-clock budget in an automation test is a flake) by `World.Scenario.WallAtScenarioScale` on every run, so these stay checkable from the log rather than from memory.

| | ms |
|---|---|
| `RunningBond` — lay the boxes and the whole graph | **1.4** |
| `BuildRunningBond` — lay + spawn 1,220 actors + `AdoptLayout` | **189.7** |
| `SolveLoads` over 1,220 pieces and 3,520 joints | **30.3** |
| `SolveAndPush` — the per-click cost | **34.2**, and **35.9** again |
| begin-play with the real game mode (world + build + solve) | **237.6** |

**The bottleneck is not where the O(pieces × connections) note predicted.** 188 of the 190 ms is **spawning actors**; the graph build is under 2 ms. So the recorded adjacency-index fix (below, under the load solver) is **not justified by this measurement on its own** — it would attack the 30 ms, not the 188. Per-click is ~35 ms, about two frames: a perceptible hitch, not a hang.

**Keep the wall at 30 × 40.** It is affordable, it is the mixed-size case (half bats at alternating course ends), and it is the only thing in the project that exercises the solver at a scale worth the name.

**The worst-loaded joint of the whole wall sits at 0.00495 of capacity** — masonry is enormously overbuilt in compression, so forty courses is still two orders of magnitude from gravity alone doing anything. That number is what says the collapse tests will have to *remove* support rather than pile weight on.

**`FStructurePiece` should take a material rather than a bare mass.** Materials own density, and `StructureTest.cpp` now derives `BrickMassKg` from `ClayBrick`'s density rather than hard-coding it — but `FStructure::AddPiece` still takes a bare `MassKg`, so every caller has to do that derivation itself. It only goes away for real when a piece knows what it is made of. Small phase-3 change; do it with the brick actor.

**Fastener profiles assume a fastener density the data cannot express.** `Nail`, `Screw` and `Bolt` are EN 1995-1-1 characteristic capacities — a force per fastener, in kN — smeared over a reference of **one fastener per 100 cm² of interface**, because the model compares stresses and has no notion of how many nails are in a joint. The assumption is documented at the top of the fastener block in `ConnectionProfiles.cpp` and every figure scales with it. When fastener count becomes a property of a joint, these should be **re-derived from the kN figures, not re-tuned**.

**Deferred: connection-to-material pairing.** A joint's real strength depends on the pair, not just the connection — a nail holds far better in oak than in pine, and mortar bonds to porous brick but not to a sealed face. The answer is **not** an N×M×N table but weakest-link: a joint fails when *either* the connection gives, *or* the bond to a face peels, *or* the material itself fails, so effective strength is the minimum of those, with bond approximated as `connection_strength × material_bond_factor`. DESIGN.md §2 already gestures at this ("the mortar gives before the brick; the nail goes before the timber") without stating it as the rule. **Build it when a second material exists to prove it** — that is DESIGN.md §4's "add wood once, only to prove the code genuinely reads the profile". Written earlier, no test could tell it from a constant.

## The load solver — outstanding, and deliberately left alone

Everything here is known and unfixed. None of it blocks the list above.

- **`FConnection` is still copyable and the latch is still per-copy.** `for (FConnection C : Connections)` — a missing `&` — compiles clean, latches every overloaded joint *on the temporary*, and leaves the real connections untouched, so the structure reports zero broken joints under any load. `SolveAndBreak` holds by reference and `Structure.Cascade` reads every assertion back through `GetConnection`, so **the trap is closed at the one site that latches today** — but nothing in the type prevents the next one. Making it non-copyable was considered and rejected: no test drives it, it would break `AddConnection(const FConnection&)`'s value semantics and every fixture builder in the suite, and the actual defence is one ampersand. Revisit if a second site ever latches.
- **`StructureFuzzSupport::UtilisationUnder` is still a hand transcription, and redirecting it at production is the next change here.** *(Raised 2026-08-02 when `FConnection::UtilisationUnder` landed.)* Production now owns a non-mutating evaluator, so the fuzz's private copy of the same arithmetic no longer has to exist — but it was deliberately **not** redirected in the same slice, and the sequencing is the point: the oracle audits the break *decision*, so pointing it at the code under test in the same change that refactored that code would make the fuzz agree with any error the refactor introduced. Do it as its own change, and re-run mutations **(g)** and **(h)** from the table above both before and after, since those are the only two that watch an over-eager break and so are the only ones that can tell a working oracle from a laundered one. Note **(b) hangs rather than fails** — run it under a timeout. The lockstep note recorded further down (five joints settle at utilisation exactly 1.0, one at 1 − 1 ulp, so one ulp of drift is five spurious failures) is the reason the transcription is exact today and the reason the redirect is worth making.
- **`CheckCascadeCase`'s `ReportedSupportedWeightUU` has a spec-vs-structure index desync one level up.** It indexes `Case.Spec.Pieces[Index]` while calling `Structure.IsPieceSupported(Index)` in the same loop. Safe today because no fixture specifies a piece that `AddPiece` rejects — but a row with a negative or NaN mass would shift structure indices off spec indices, and every per-piece assertion would then silently describe the *wrong piece* while still passing or failing plausibly. The connection-level version of this was fixed (the runner now walks `Structure.NumConnections()` and takes handles from `GetConnection`, which are validated at the door); this is the same bug one level up. Closing it needs a decision about what such a row should assert, so it is its own red test rather than a drive-by.
- **A degenerate fixture row must name the UNGROUNDED piece first, or it cannot reach the defect.** The harness bounds bug was originally reported with `{ 0, INDEX_NONE, ... }`, which passes green: `||` short-circuits, piece 0 is grounded, and the second handle is never evaluated. `{ 1, INDEX_NONE, ... }` is what actually reached it — `Array index out of bounds: -1`, process exit 3, the run aborted after 15 of 27 tests. Recorded so nobody re-derives a row that cannot fire.
- **`UtilisationTolerance`'s `1e-12` absolute floor is a constraint, not a taste.** The tolerance is relative `1e-9` with that floor, and the floor exists only for rows whose expectation is an exact zero. It must stay well below the smallest *non-zero* utilisation expectation in the file — currently `Unbreakable`'s 1.96e-11 — or that row stops being able to fail at all, which is what it was before (an absolute 1e-9 slack could not distinguish 1.96e-11 from zero, and zero is exactly what a joint dropped out of the load path reports). Noted beside the constant.
- **`StructureCascadeTest.cpp` still hard-sets `BrickMassKg = 2.72`, and it was deliberately left.** `StructureTest.cpp` now derives its brick mass from `ClayBrick`'s density, so `Profiles.MaterialInvariants` is the single external anchor for the figure. The cascade file's copy is used **only** for grounded pads and piers, whose weight terminates at the earth and never reaches a joint, so no expectation in that file depends on it and no expectation would change if it were derived. Deriving it is a two-line tidy whenever the file is next opened; it is not a drift risk today because nothing reads it into an assertion.

- **`AddConnection` will happily join a REMOVED piece, and that connection is never severed.** *(Found 2026-08-01 implementing `RemovePiece`; deliberately left, because closing it is branching logic and needs its own red test first.)* A tombstoned slot is still a **valid array index**, so `AddConnection`'s `Pieces.IsValidIndex` guard passes for a piece that has gone. Removal severs the joints that exist *at the moment it is called*, so a joint added afterwards is intact and in the support relation, naming a piece that is not there. It is the fail-open direction, and worse than a dangling handle: if the live end is grounded, the reachability walk marks the **dead** piece supported, and `IsPieceSupported` then reports a removed piece as held up — exactly the answer the `bIsInTheStructure` root guard exists to prevent. Unreachable today because nothing calls `AddConnection` after `RemovePiece`, and it stays that way only as long as structures are built once and then knocked about; a scenario that adds bracing to a damaged wall would reach it immediately. The fix is one `GraphValidation` row (a connection naming a removed piece, expected rejected) and one guard, and it should reuse `IsPieceRemoved` rather than testing the flag again.
- **`GetBreakPass` and `HasGiven` can be made to disagree, through the front door.** `FConnection::ApplyForce` is public and the latch is a copyable member, so `FConnection C; C.ApplyForce(huge); Structure.AddConnection(C);` stores an **already-given** joint whose `ConnectionBreakPass` is `INDEX_NONE`. `GetBreakPass`'s contract says `INDEX_NONE` means "still intact", which is then a lie: the joint is invisible to the whole solver while `NumConnections()` still counts it. No caller does this today. The obvious near-term one is **save/load restoring a partly-collapsed structure**, and phase 5 replays exactly these stamps. Closing it is either a `GraphValidation` row (reject a connection that has already given) or a deliberate accept-and-stamp; either needs its own red test first.
- **Phase 6's collapse test now has the precondition it needs, and must actually use it.** `FStructure::GetPieceSupport` exists (`Grounded` / `Supported` / `Falling` / `Stranded`), so DESIGN.md §4's "pull bricks until it topples, confirm it falls at the predicted number" can distinguish a wall that fell from **load** from one the solver merely could not route around a loop — the outcome is identical either way, so without the check that test would pass, stay green through a retune, and be calibrated against the number of removals that makes a cycle. Three bricks out of the bottom course of a five-brick wall is enough to produce one. **Assert no piece is `Stranded` at the moment of collapse.**
- **A cycle in the support relation is reported, not solved.** Pieces caught in an unroutable knot — and only those; stranding is local, pinned by `Structure.StrandingIsLocal` and `Structure.StrandingPropagatesUpward` — are marked **unsupported**, so the stall is visible from outside and conservation holds. `Structure.SupportCycle` pins this, with a two-brick and a three-brick gap, plus two controls (a single grounded neighbour, and a cycle through a grounded piece that still resolves). What is still missing is any *rule for how load divides round a loop*: a course spanning a wide gap is reported as falling rather than as arching.

  **Deliberately kept for later, not dropped** *(reaffirmed 2026-08-02)*. The conservative answer is closer to right than it first looks: in real masonry, bricks over an opening with nothing beneath them **do** fall, which is why doorways have lintels — and with a lintel there is no cycle at all, since the bricks above simply rest on it through ordinary bed joints. The case where this model is genuinely wrong is a **true arch**: wedge-shaped voussoirs carrying load around a curve in compression, standing with no lintel at all. That is a real thing to want eventually and a proper piece of engineering design — how load divides around a loop — but nothing in the first wall needs it. Until then the honest fix is to make the *reason* observable (see the entry above) so a test can tell a real collapse from a solver limitation. See DESIGN.md §4's shear test, which is the scenario that will want the full rule.
- **The fixpoint loop is load-bearing, and exactly one test says so.** `SolveLoads` iterates because stranding changes who reaches the ground, so a piece resting *only* on the knot must itself come out unsupported — and the pass that stranded the knot has already written that piece's weight onto its joint, because it is a leaf and Kahn seeds with it first. Only the re-solve wipes that. **`Structure.StrandingPropagatesUpward`** is what discriminates the loop from a single pass; delete the loop and its first case reports the brick supported with **2667.2 uu** on a joint whose far end is falling. Don't "simplify" the loop away without checking that test still fails when you do.
- **A joint carries one force spread evenly over its whole area — there are no levers, and no bending.** *(Raised 2026-07-31. Advanced, deliberately not being tackled now.)* `ComputeUtilisation` divides a force by an interface area and gets **one** stress, uniform across the joint. Real loads are rarely centred, and an off-centre load on a joint is a **lever**: it produces a moment, and the stress across the interface becomes a gradient rather than a constant — highest compression at the edge the piece pivots about, falling away across the joint, and going into **tension** at the far edge, which lifts.

  The worked example is a brick overhanging its support with load on the projecting end. Today the model sees a bed joint under a downward force and reports modest, uniform compression — comfortably safe. In reality the brick pivots on the outer edge of its bearing: compression spikes there while the inner edge is pulled open. **That is the edge that governs**, because mortar holds roughly 10 MPa in compression and about 0.1 MPa in tension — a hundred to one. So the joint peels from the back while the model reports it barely loaded. Same signature as every other defect this subsystem has produced: a plausible number, and a piece that is not really being held.

  This is why real masonry limits how far a course may corbel out, and it is the same mechanism that makes arches and vaults work — thrust kept inside the joint so no part of it opens.

  **What makes it hard is that the lever arm does not exist in our data.** Pieces have no position and joints have no extent, only an area, so there is nothing to measure eccentricity *from*. Closing it needs, at minimum: where the load is applied relative to the joint's centroid; the joint's extent and axis, not just its area (a section modulus); a moment term alongside compression / shear / tension in `FConnectionLoad`; and `ComputeUtilisation` computing **edge** stresses rather than an average. It is therefore blocked behind the same missing spatial layer as force delivery — see the graph-representation section.

  Note the knock-on: today gravity through an axis-aligned bed joint never produces tension, which several tests and comments are carefully scoped around. Once moments exist, gravity produces tension **routinely**, and that scoping becomes wrong rather than merely narrow.
- **The bed/head tier bakes in gravity as the only load direction.** "Substantially vertical" is `|normal.Z| > cos 45°`, which is meaningless once explosions and kinetic impacts arrive and the load no longer points down — routing has to generalise to the direction of the applied load. Flagged in DESIGN.md §3 as a known limitation of the model, not just of this code. The 45° line itself is pinned from both sides at 40° and 50° by `Structure.SupportTierThreshold`; the exact tie is deliberately unasserted, which is what makes `BedJointCosine` safe to spell as a `constexpr` literal — only a normal at *exactly* 45.000° could tell it from `1.0 / FMath::Sqrt(2.0)`. **The test file spells the same literal**, because `SpecSupportsOf` is the oracle for the `DegenerateInputs` matrix and has to agree with `GetJointRole` bit for bit; change one and change the other.
- **A tilted joint ABOVE a piece supports it in tension, and that is accepted.** `GetJointRole` tests `|NormalZTowardPiece| > cos45` before it looks at the sign, so the head tier is sign-blind: an inclined face over a piece is still that piece's fallback support, and holding it up means pulling the face open. Characterised by `Structure.TiltedJointClassification` — at 50° a 2.7216 kg brick gives compression 0 / shear 2043.20 / **tension 1714.44**. DESIGN.md §3 states this outcome and accepts it, so **do not "fix" it**; it is why the 45° line looks like a cliff and is not one (just below it the same face is `BedAbove`, bears nothing, and the piece falls instead). Two comments in `StructureTest.cpp` used to read as properties of the *model* — "tension is always zero", "a gravity load path never pulls a joint open" — and are properties of the *fixtures*, every one of which is axis-aligned; both are now scoped, and adding a tilted normal to the `DegenerateInputs` matrix will turn its tension row red as a discovery rather than a regression. Note the design's "fails almost at once" is about the *ratio* of mortar's tensile to compressive limit, not about this load: one brick over 100 cm² reaches only 0.017 utilisation and breaks nothing.
- **Neither fuzz compares the stranded set both ways yet, and the seam that lets them now exists.** *(Rewritten 2026-08-02 when `GetPieceSupport` landed; the seam is no longer the blocker, writing the assertion is.)* Both fuzzes still compare only the composite answer in both directions and the oracle's cycle set one way (a piece on a cycle must be reported unsupported), because that is all `IsPieceSupported` could see. `GetPieceSupport` exposes production's stranded set directly, so each fuzz can assert **set equality** between the oracle's cycle set and the pieces reading `Stranded` — and that promotes `STRANDING` and `DOWNWARD STRANDING` from diagnostics (recorded below as subsumed by `SUPPORT`) into properties that can fire alone. It is a test change with no production change behind it, and per the house rule it needs a mutation to prove it bites; the obvious one is stranding on un-orderability, which is already recorded as live for `Structure.Fuzz`.
- **`SolveLoads`'s pass bound is provably 2, not `NumPieces + 1`, and nothing asserts it.** Stranding only removes nodes and edges, and removal cannot create a cycle, so a second pass can never strand anything the first did not — the third exists only to observe that and stop. Measured max across 8,500 fuzzed graphs up to 9 pieces: 2. Left unasserted because `SolveLoads` returns `void` and there is no seam to observe a pass count through; adding one is a production change and needs its own red test. Worth doing if the per-solve cost ever matters, since the bound is what multiplies the O(pieces × connections) figure below.
- **The OUTER cascade depth measures ≤ 4, against a documented bound of `NumConnections`, and nothing asserts the tighter figure either.** Companion to the item above, with one difference that matters: `SolveAndBreak` **returns** its pass count, so unlike the inner fixpoint this bound is already observable and would need no production change to assert. Measured across the cascade fuzz's 8,000 breakable structures: deepest 4. It is not asserted because 4 is a property of *this generator* rather than of the algorithm — a tuning-sensitive number that would fail on the day somebody widened the mass table for an unrelated reason. If it is ever worth pinning, pin it as a generous sanity ceiling, not at 4.
- **Multi-pass cascades are only 6.1% of what the cascade fuzz generates** — 491 structures of 8,000, against 2,157 that break in one pass and 5,352 that stand as built. That fraction is what the multi-pass floor guards, and 491 was enough for three separate mutations (c, e and the load-path half of a) to knock it to zero, so it is sufficient rather than comfortable. Raising it means biasing topology toward pieces with **several parallel supports of differing profile** — that is the shape where breaking one joint moves a share onto a neighbour that cannot take it. Deliberately not done: it is a generator change, and the existing rate already demonstrates redistribution.
- **Neither fuzz covers PIECE REMOVAL, and that is a deliberate ordering decision rather than an oversight. `RemovePiece` is now implemented and green, so this is the NEXT change — the reason for the wait has expired.** *(Raised 2026-08-01 while writing `Tests/StructureRemovalTest.cpp`.)* Removal is topological — it changes who reaches the ground, who may take a share, and which joints are in the relation at all — which is exactly the shape both real solver defects lived in, so it belongs in a property test with an independent oracle rather than in more example rows. It was **not** written as part of the red step for three reasons, all about the red step being useless rather than about the property being unwanted:
  - With `RemovePiece` stubbed, a generator that removes pieces removes nothing, so the property is **vacuously green**; write the oracle assuming removal worked and all 8,000 cases fail identically, printing no information the five named tests do not already give. Neither is a usable red signal.
  - The value of the oracle is entirely in being **derived differently**: production is expected to express removal by latching the touching joints and letting the existing `HasGiven` filter do the rest, so the oracle must instead **drop the node and its edges outright** — including dropping `bIsGrounded`, since a removed grounded piece is no longer ground. That divergence is only meaningful once there is a real implementation to disagree with.
  - The house rule is that any property added to either fuzz is **proven to bite by mutation**, and mutations can only be run against working code. Note mutation (b) **hangs** rather than fails, so run under a timeout.

  **Removal is green as of 2026-08-01, so do it now**, in `Structure.CascadeFuzz` (extend the existing generator and `StructureFuzzSupport` oracle; do not start a third harness). Shape: remove `0..2` seeded random pieces before cascading, floor the number of cases that actually remove something so a generator that stopped removing fails rather than passing in silence, and add the two properties the named tests assert only on hand-written shapes — **a joint touching a removed piece has given and carries no pass stamp**, and **a joint that has given without a stamp touches a removed piece**.

  **Add a third property now that stamps are global**: cascade, remove, cascade again, and require every stamp written by the second call to be strictly greater than every stamp the first call wrote — with the first call's stamps captured before the removal so nothing rewritten can hide. `Structure.BreakPassesContinueAcrossCalls` pins that on two hand-built shapes only, and the numbering is exactly the kind of arithmetic a fuzz is better at than examples. It needs no production change; the mutation to prove it bites is restarting the pass counter at 1, which is the defect that made this necessary.

- **`CascadeFuzz` does not check that a joint broke in the EARLIEST pass it deserved to.** The broke-under-capacity sweep is one-directional — it catches a break that was not deserved, and `OVER CAPACITY` catches a cascade that stopped a pass early — but nothing catches a joint that gave in pass 3 having been over capacity already in pass 2's solve. The per-pass rebuild the sweep already does makes this reachable with no production change: sweep *every* joint present in the pass-N graph and require the ones over capacity to be stamped exactly N, since DESIGN.md §3 breaks all of them in the same pass. Left for its own change because it is a different property in the opposite direction and would need its own mutation to show it bites. `Structure.Cascade`'s hand-derived utilisations (a joint that must HOLD at 0.30, another at 0.60) are what pin the order meanwhile.
- **`CascadeFuzz` mostly probes the obliterated regime, not the marginal one.** Utilisation at the moment of break: median **9.9x** capacity, p75 51x, p95 660x, max 1.25e4, and 11.5% infinite (dry stone in tension — its tensile strength is exactly 0.0). Only 13.7% of breaks are below 1.5x and 20% below 2x. That is still ~790 marginal breaks, which is why the fuzz finds anything at all, but the regime where redistribution is subtle — a joint just over the line, a share that only just tips a neighbour — is a minority of the coverage, and it is exactly where an off-by-a-little defect would hide. Biasing the mass table toward the marginal band would be the cheapest improvement available here; it is a generator change and would renumber nothing (each case is seeded from its own index), but it moves every reported distribution figure, so do it deliberately rather than in passing.

  **The ranges themselves are defensible** and this is the answer to "isn't 25 MPa on a mortar joint physically absurd": capping every mass row at 0.3 MPa nominal still gives 3,319 breaks and cascade depth 3, so the properties are not hostage to the extreme rows. Breaks spread across all five breakable profiles and all five area buckets.
- **`CascadeFuzz`'s `TERMINATION` bound only ever fires through the jointless-structure edge**, and is therefore the weakest of its properties. The assertion is `0 <= Passes <= JointCount`; with joints present, no mutation tried produced a count *above* the joint count — non-termination in this code presents as a **hang**, not as a wrong number, so the loop never returns to be checked (see mutation (b)). It is kept because it is the documented contract from DESIGN.md §3 and costs nothing, but treat it as a contract statement rather than as evidence, and don't count it when judging coverage.
- **Three of `CascadeFuzz`'s properties are diagnostics rather than independent properties, and cannot be made to fire alone.** Each was proved subsumed by reading the oracle, so **do not spend time raising `MaxReportedFailures` under a mutation hoping to see them** — that task cannot resolve:
  - **`STRANDING`** — in the oracle a cycle-stranded piece is skipped by the propagation loop and can never be grounded (the `Reaches` adjacency excludes grounded pieces on both axes), so `bStrandedInCycle[i]` implies `!bSupported[i]`. Production reporting such a piece held up is therefore *also* a `SUPPORT` mismatch, on the same piece in the same iteration.
  - **`DOWNWARD STRANDING`** — its precondition (a grounded piece among `Supports[Index]`, `Index` not itself cycle-stranded) makes the oracle's least fixed point mark the piece supported, so production saying "falling" is exactly a `SUPPORT` mismatch.
  - **`NON-FINITE`** — for a survivor, `IsNearlyEqual` is false against NaN or Inf so `LOAD` fires; for a broken joint, the zero check is false so `BROKEN CARRIES LOAD` fires.

  All three are **kept deliberately**: they name the fault far better than the general property does, and a message saying "piece 3 is on a cycle and production reports it held up" is worth a lot more to whoever reads it than a bare support mismatch. Treat them as labels on a failure the suite would catch anyway, not as coverage.

  **`NOT VERTICAL` is the exception and genuinely earns its place.** `LOAD` compares only `.Z`, so `NOT VERTICAL` is the only assertion in *either* fuzz that looks at X or Y at all — a solver that pushed load along the interface normal rather than down would produce plausible magnitudes, entirely the wrong direction, and nothing else would see it.
- **An unsupported island reports zero load on its internal joints.** Defensible for a static solver — nothing is holding it up, so there is no static load path — but it means "carries nothing" is ambiguous between *given*, *grounded on both sides* and *falling*. If a strain readout ever needs to tell those apart, it needs more than the force.
- **The solver is O(pieces x connections), and the knot check adds a walk per piece.** Building the support lists scans the whole connection array once per piece; replacing it with an adjacency index built once per solve is the obvious fix (it is outside the fixpoint loop, so it stays a single scan). Inside the loop, each pass redoes reachability, the load-path filter, the accumulation, and then `LoadReturnsToPiece` **once per supported ungrounded piece** — each a fresh walk with its own `TArray<bool>` visited buffer, so step five alone is O(pieces x (pieces + edges)). One Tarjan SCC pass would give the same answer in linear time, and a single reusable visited buffer would kill the allocations; both were left undone because nothing measures slow yet. A structure that strands nothing still pays for the walks. Fine at MVP size and deliberately not optimised; flag rather than fix until something measures slow.

  **This got more valuable with 2b, and was still deliberately not done there.** `SolveAndBreak` runs a *complete* `SolveLoads` per breaking pass, so the whole figure above is now multiplied by the number of joints that give — a collapsing wall re-scans the connection array once per piece, once per pass, once per break. The adjacency index remains the obvious fix and remains a pure performance refactor with no test driving it; doing it inside the cascade change would have mixed a behaviour change with a rewrite of the solver's internals.

  **IT IS NOW MEASURED, AND THE MEASUREMENT DOES NOT JUSTIFY IT** *(2026-08-04)*. At scenario scale — 1,220 pieces, 3,520 joints — a complete `SolveLoads` takes **30.3 ms** and `SolveAndPush` **34.2 ms**, against **189.7 ms** for `BuildRunningBond`, of which **188 ms is spawning 1,220 actors**. So the solver is about a sixth of the cost of standing the wall up and roughly two frames per click; the expensive thing is the world, not the graph. Figures and their source in the MVP section. **Two caveats keep this open rather than closing it**: nothing has yet measured a *cascade* at this scale (the multiplier above is per breaking pass, and the wall sits at 0.005 of capacity so nothing breaks under gravity alone), and the wall is one structure — the figure is not per-frame. Revisit when something measures a cascade slow, not before.
- **`AddConnection`'s normal guard has a top-end hole.** A normal with components near 1e200 gives an infinite `SizeSquared`, `InvSqrt(inf)` is 0, so `FVector::Normalize()` returns **true** having produced the zero vector — and `GetJointRole` then classifies that as a head joint, which is a support tier. Unreachable from real content, and left alone deliberately: closing it is branching logic, so it needs a red `GraphValidation` row (a huge-component normal, expected rejected) before the `IsFinite` check goes in.

  **The same hole is noted in `FConnection::UtilisationUnder` (the normalise block, which moved there from `ApplyForce` when the query landed — grep the comment rather than trusting a line number), and fixing it there is a LOCKSTEP change.** `StructureFuzzSupport::UtilisationUnder` transcribes that normalisation deliberately, because both of its callers audit the break *decision* and so must measure against the capacity the decision itself used — five joints in the cascade fuzz settle at utilisation exactly 1.0 and one more at 1−1ulp, so a single ulp of upward drift is five spurious `OVER CAPACITY` failures. Change one and change the other in the same commit; the function carries the instruction in its doc block, as `BedJointCosine` does.
- **`AddConnection` stores the interface normal exactly as given, non-unit included.** It only checks the normal *can* be normalised; `FConnection::ApplyForce` normalises its own copy each time it is called. Correct, and asserted by the "a non-unit interface normal" acceptance row, but it means the stored normal is not canonical — anything that compares normals directly must normalise first.
- **DESIGN.md §4 never mentions property fuzzing or the independent-oracle technique, and it should.** Grep DESIGN.md for "fuzz" or "oracle" and there is nothing: §4 "Testing Strategy" describes unit-vs-integration, gravity on/off, assert-on-mechanism-vs-outcome and the test catalogue, and stops there. So the single most productive technique this project has — generating thousands of random graphs and checking them against a second implementation derived by different algorithms, which found both of the solver's real defects and now guards every break decision in the cascade — is described **only in this file**, which by its own definition lists what is *not* done. That is backwards: it is architecture, so it belongs in the architecture doc, with this file left to carry only the running state (case counts, timings, the mutation table). Deliberately not written in the round that noticed it, because that round was closing a coverage gap and writing a DESIGN.md section is its own change with its own review.
- **DESIGN.md's geometry-collection story is now correct and settled** *(2026-08-01, was: §3's opening bullet credited collapse to "Chaos strain-based damage")*. §3's collapse bullet, §2's base-destructible-actor item and the Chaos-strain callout all now say we compute the loads and decide the breaks ourselves, and that Chaos takes over only once a piece is released. §3 gained "Geometry collections: complementary, not an alternative", recording that they answer *what one piece looks like as it dies* rather than *which piece gives way*, that adopting them structurally would trade directional strength for a scalar threshold, and that `RemovePiece` is the seam if per-piece fracturing is added later. It also records the thing most often assumed to be the blocker and is not: **force delivery is not what blocks sideways loads — gravity-specific routing is.**

## The layout producer — outstanding, and deliberately left alone

All three were found while implementing or testing `Core/Layout` and left because closing any of them is branching logic with no failing test behind it. The first is a `MakeInterface`-as-an-API hole; **the other two are reachable through `RunningBond`'s own spec**, which validates less than it looks like it does — see the third bullet, which used to claim the opposite.

- **An extent of `TNumericLimits<double>::Max()` still produces an infinite interface area, and `IsUsableBox` cannot see it.** *(Split off 2026-08-02 when the `+inf` extent hole was closed; **do not confuse the two**.)* `IsUsableBox` now checks `FMath::IsFinite` on the extent as well as the centre, which refuses a **real IEEE `+inf`** — `Layout.Interface`'s "an infinite extent is not a joint" row covers exactly that and is green. `DBL_MAX` is a different case wearing the same clothes: it is **finite**, so it sails through an `IsFinite` guard and through `!(Extent > 0.0)`, that axis still overlaps by ~1.8e308 and so is never the separation axis, and if the other two axes form a face the joint is **accepted with an area that overflowed to `+inf`** (10.25 x 1.797e308) — the same fail-open outcome, reached by arithmetic rather than by a bad input value. So the earlier version of this bullet, which suggested `1e308`/`DBL_MAX` as the row to write against an `IsFinite` fix, was **wrong**: that row would not have gone green on this change. Closing it is a **different branch** — a finiteness check on the **computed** `AreaSqCm`, after the multiply — and therefore needs its own red `Layout.Interface` row (an extent of `TNumericLimits<double>::Max()` on one axis, expected refused **and** left with a zero area, since both halves of that loop must fire). Still not reachable from a structure: `FStructure::AddConnection` rejects a non-finite area, so today it is a wrong return value rather than a corrupt graph.
- **`RunningBond` does not clear `OutLayout` before writing to it.** Every caller today passes a fresh `FBrickLayout`, and `Layout.RunningBond`'s rejection rows check "a refused spec must leave no boxes/pieces/joints behind" against fresh ones — so a **reused** layout would accumulate a second wall on top of the first, and a rejected spec would leave the previous wall's contents behind while returning false. Whether it should clear or should refuse a non-empty out-param is a decision, not an omission, which is why it needs its own red row rather than a drive-by. Note `FStructure` has no `Reset`, so "clear" means assigning a fresh one.

  **`AdoptLayout` now has the identical question about a non-empty `Out` binding, and it was deliberately left open for the same reason** *(2026-08-03)*. It appends, so adopting into a binding that already holds a wall stacks a second one on top of it with handles that still line up but a structure id that names both. Nothing does that today — every caller passes a fresh binding. **Answer the two together**, since answering one in isolation pre-empts the other and they should not end up with opposite policies.
- **`RunningBond` ignores what `AddPiece` and `AddConnection` return.** `Boxes` is appended unconditionally, so a rejected piece desyncs the box array from the handle range and breaks `FBrickLayout`'s one-box-per-handle contract silently.

  **It is reachable, through `RunningBond`'s own spec, and that is a correction** *(noticed 2026-08-02 while closing the `+inf` extent hole; deliberately left, because closing it is branching logic with no failing test)*. This bullet used to say "unreachable today — the spec guards make every mass positive and finite". They do not: the dimension guard is `!(Spec.BrickSizeCm[Axis] > 0.0)`, and **`+inf > 0.0` is true**, so an infinite brick dimension passes it exactly as an infinite extent passed `IsUsableBox`. `PieceMassKg` then answers with a mass `FStructure::AddPiece` refuses — the infinite extent lands inside `IsUsableBox`, so the refusal is now a deliberate NaN rather than an overflowed infinity, and the outcome downstream is identical (`INDEX_NONE`) — and `LayBrick` adds the box and returns `INDEX_NONE` as though it were a handle — so `Boxes` is one longer than the piece array, every later handle is off by one against its box, and `JoinIfTouching` indexes `Layout.Boxes[INDEX_NONE]`. `RunningBond` still returns **true**. No caller supplies an infinite dimension today, which is the only thing keeping it quiet. The fix is a `Layout.RunningBond` rejection row (an infinite `BrickSizeCm` component, expected refused with nothing written) plus `FMath::IsFinite` on the spec guard — and separately, honouring the handle `LayBrick` returns instead of assuming it is valid.

## The piece action table — outstanding, and deliberately left alone

All of these were found or consciously left while making `Tests/PieceActionsTest.cpp` green *(2026-08-03)*. None has a failing test behind it, and two of them are open **questions** rather than known defects — they are recorded so the answer arrives with a test rather than as a drive-by.

> **The `CanRun`-at-commit question is ANSWERED** *(2026-08-03)*: `RunPieceAction` consults it and refuses before running. The reasoning is in the `Core/PieceActions` Snapshot entry and in the header; the short version is that leaving the commit door wider than the menu makes the rule depend on which door you came through, and every future caller — a keybind, an RPC, a replay — silently gets the permissive one. Driven by `Core.PieceActions.CommitRespectsCanRun`. **`Delete`'s `CanRun` is now the single statement of when deleting is allowed**, so widening it is how a future "yes, you may delete falling debris" gets made, not a second policy at the commit site.

- **A load helper for hard content references was NOT built, and it is a trap rather than an omission.** The obvious `LoadRequiredAsset<T>(Path)` — an `FObjectFinder` plus an `ensureAlwaysMsgf` naming the path — has no test behind its null branch, and worse, **the naive implementation is silently catastrophic**: `ConstructorHelpers::FObjectFinder` is normally a `static` local, and a function-local static inside a **template** is one object per instantiation, shared by every call with the same `T`. So all four `UInputAction` call sites would resolve to whichever asset was asked for first, and **`Content.RequiredAssetsResolve` would stay green** — all four references non-null, all four paths listed. What was done instead is the cheap half with no branch in it: the paths are named constants in `RequiredContent.h` and the constructors spell them from there, so there is one copy of each string. If the helper is ever wanted, it needs its own red test first, and that test has to assert the four actions resolve to **four different assets** — which nothing in the suite does today. (`ensure` over `check` remains the right call for a *content* fault: halting the editor means you cannot open the tool you need to fix it in, a fired ensure is already counted as a test error, and both are compiled out in Shipping anyway.)
- **What `ActorToDestroy` holds for an action that destroys nothing is whatever fell out, and what fell out is the piece's still-live actor.** The rule implemented is "hand back the captured actor if `Run` reported it did something", which is right for `Delete` and is asserted; the tripwire, which runs successfully and removes nothing, therefore gets handed the actor of a brick **still standing in the wall** — and a caller that destroys what comes back would tear it out of the world. The obvious answers are to gate the hand-back on the piece actually having gone (`IsPieceRemoved` after the run), or to have the row itself nominate what it orphaned via `FPieceActionContext`. **Decide it when the second row lands**, with a red test, since the second row is the first thing that can be wrong about it.
- **A row with a null `Run` — or now a null `CanRun` — crashes `RunPieceAction`, and a crash aborts the whole automation run rather than failing a test.** `TableIsWellFormed` sweeps the shipped table for both pointers, so no row in `AllPieceActions()` can be malformed, and `PieceActionsFor` iterates only that table so it needs no check of its own — but `RunPieceAction` takes an `FPieceAction&` from anywhere, and it now calls **both** `Action.CanRun` and `Action.Run` with no check. Left alone because a guard is branching logic with no failing test, and the test file already avoids the equivalent hazard on its own side (`RequireAction` bails loudly rather than dereferencing a null row). One red row closes it: commit a locally-built `FPieceAction{ TEXT("x"), &Something, nullptr }` against a live piece, expected to report it did nothing.
- **`CommitRefFailsClosed`'s refusal rows are a CHAINED red — but the door guard has now been mutated, and it bites hard.** All nine ref-shapes passed vacuously against the stub, exactly like `AdoptLayout`'s refusal rows (recorded under Conventions and gotchas), so their passing before implementation was evidence of nothing. The live mutation, for whoever next touches the guard: in `RunPieceAction`, replace `Binding.ResolvePiece(Ref)` with `Ref.PieceIndex`. **43 assertion failures**, all in `CommitRefFailsClosed`; `CanRunFiltersTheMenu` and `Delete` stay green, which is correct — neither goes through the door. The failures are worth knowing the shape of, because they are two distinct bugs: `bRan` reporting true, plus **the tripwire's counter reporting `Run` was entered**, which is what separates "refused before running" from "ran, then reported nothing". The removed-piece row (`{7, 2}`) is the sharp one — a tombstoned slot is a valid array index, so `Delete` still refuses it (`RemovePiece` guards) while the tripwire runs, and only the counter sees it.
- **`MenuOffersWhatCanRun`'s rejection rows were the same chained red, and the door guard HAS been mutated** *(2026-08-03)*. All twelve ref-shapes passed vacuously against the stub, which returned an empty array. The live mutation, for whoever next touches `PieceActionsFor`: replace `Binding.ResolvePiece(Ref)` with `Ref.PieceIndex`. **6 assertion failures**, all in `MenuOffersWhatCanRun`, and they are exactly the two rows a bare index cannot tell apart from a handle — *a brick belonging to a DIFFERENT structure* `{8, 0}` and *a ref with no structure id* `{-1, 0}`, each firing the offers-Delete row, the exact-row-count row and the offered-a-row-whose-CanRun-says-no row. **The other rejection rows deliberately stay green under it, and that is the shape rather than a gap**: the removed and out-of-range rows are caught downstream by `Delete`'s own `CanRun` (`FStructure::IsPieceRemoved` answers true for a handle naming nothing), and the default-ref row by the `INDEX_NONE` guard the mutation leaves in place. `World.Click` also stays green, because its miss cases carry a default ref. ## The piece menu presenter — outstanding, and deliberately left alone

The input binding, the presenter's state, the choice, the widget and **both halves of highlighting** have all landed *(2026-08-04)*, so the entries that used to head this section — nothing calls `BindAction`, the cursor/free-look conflict, nothing draws, nothing can be chosen, nothing reads `EBrickHighlight`, nothing calls `HoverAlongRay` — are gone; all of it is described under the presenter and the brick actor in Snapshot. **What is left below is one feature that was asked for and not built, one recorded exception, two things nobody has confirmed in a running game, one product decision nobody has taken, and one measured cost nobody has measured.**

- **THE PER-BRICK BREAKOUT'S DATA HAS LANDED; NOTHING DRAWS IT YET** *(requested 2026-08-04; `Core/PieceInspection` landed 2026-08-05)*. What was asked for is a menu listing **every selected brick** as its own entry, where hovering one singles that brick out with its own highlight, leaving the rest of the selection lit as it was, and shows **per joint** the force it is carrying and its utilisation. The **model half is now in and tested** — see `Core/PieceInspection` in Snapshot — so what remains is the presenter: the per-brick entries, the hover, and the wording and colouring. Every number they will show is now decided somewhere a test can reach, which is the condition the widget was landed under.

  **The drift to watch for is closed rather than merely warned about.** `FStructure::GetConnectionForce` and `GetConnectionUtilisation` were already covered, and the latter is deliberately one line delegating to `FConnection::UtilisationUnder` so a readout cannot become a third hand-copy of the break decision; the breakout reads both back rather than recomputing either, and `PieceInspection.JointBreakout` asserts agreement with **exact `==`** so a re-derivation that agrees to nine decimals still fails. The bed/head tier was the one classification that would have had to be copied, and it was **moved** instead: `FStructure::GetJointRole` is the same code `SolveLoads` routes by. **Adjacency is still a scan** of the whole connection array — fine for one brick at a player's click, and the same index recorded under the load solver as measured **not** worth building for the solve.

  **The anchor is already designed and is the reason `FPieceMenuRow` carries `Refs` at all.** `Ref` is `Refs.Last()`, retained as the derived last-added member precisely so a per-brick readout has something to hang off; a breakout that remembered which brick it was showing, separately from the rows, is the stale-field bug `FPieceMenuRow` was shaped to prevent. **A third highlight state is the likely need** — "the one you are inspecting" against "the ones you have picked" — which lands on `EBrickHighlight` and `HighlightForPiece`, the one function every route already asks, and not on a second record.

  **And newtons were the presenter's decision, not Core's — TAKEN 2026-08-05.** `FJointInspection::ForceUu` stays in raw Unreal force units, deliberately: `ForceUnitsPerMPaSqCm` is 10,000 — the newton factor times cm²→mm² — so it is *not* the newton conversion, and dividing by 100 in Core would be a second open-coded boundary. The conversion lives beside the formatting as `DestructionPresenter::ForceUnitsPerNewton`, named once, and the test spells `100.0` out independently rather than importing it.

  **THE PRESENTED MODEL HAS NOW LANDED TOO, AND ONLY THE WIDGET AND THE HOVER ARE LEFT** *(2026-08-05)*. `BuildPieceMenuInspector` (`Core/PieceMenu.cpp`, driven by `Presenter.PieceMenuInspector` and `Presenter.PieceMenuJointReadout`) turns a binding plus a selection into a finished readout: the count and its text, one entry per selected brick **in pick order**, and for at most one of them that brick's joints in newtons and per cent, **each as a whole line of text**. Four decisions are load-bearing.

  **It is a SIBLING of the rows, not fields on `FPieceMenuRow`, and the deciding case is in the test.** A row is a command; an inspector is a readout, and they have opposite fail-closed polarities — `BuildPieceMenuRows` refuses the *whole* list when one ref names nothing, while a readout must still report the hole. The discriminating fixture is a selection holding a **released** brick: `PieceActionsFor`'s intersection empties and the action menu builds **zero** rows, while the inspector must still list both bricks and break out the live one's joints. An inspector carried on a row goes dark exactly when the player is asking why. That surface is also where the open product decision at rank 0 would become legible.

  **The strings are in the model on purpose**, including the singular/plural of "1 brick selected", the tier wording and the decimal places — because the widget was landed under a recorded exception whose condition is that it contains **no logic**, and a debugger readout is precisely the feature that erodes that. The price is that retuning wording is a test edit; the alternative is an unasserted `Text` and untested wording.

  **"At most one brick is inspected" holds by construction**, via an index lookup into the selection rather than a guard, which is what survives a duplicate ref. And **support text asks `bHasSupportAnswer` before it reads the state**, so a never-solved wall reads "not solved yet" rather than as a column of falling bricks — the polarity inversion recorded under Conventions and gotchas, met head-on at a new seam.

  **AN ENTRY IS LABELLED `brick <StructureId>:<PieceIndex>`, ALWAYS, AND THE TOTALITY IS THE POINT** *(decided 2026-08-05)*. The first version was `brick %d` on the piece index alone, asserted by nothing — deleting the line left the suite fully green — and the table already built the case that breaks it: a selection of `{4,1}` and `{9,1}` presented **two different bricks as the identical string "brick 1"**, one of which resolves to nothing. This is a *debugger*, so unambiguous beats friendly, and a rule that qualified only "foreign" refs would need to know which structure is the home one — state the model does not have and a branch it should not grow. A malformed ref therefore reads `brick 4:-1`: the absent half prints as `-1`, which is not a piece index any wall can have, rather than being prettied into something that reads like a real brick.

  **Two of the four gaps below are now closed**, both by rows that were green on arrival and were therefore **proved to bite by mutation** rather than by a red step:

  - **A duplicate ref that is ALSO the inspected one** — the only case that makes "at most one entry is inspected, by construction" falsifiable. The claim rests on finding membership as an index rather than comparing per entry; the readable refactor (`Entry.bIsInspected = (Entry.Ref == InspectedRef)`) passes everything else and flags **two** entries, which a widget would draw as two joint lists for one brick. The row fires on both halves — the count and the position of the marker.
  - **`EPieceSupport::Stranded → "stranded"`**, driven by a three-piece knot added to the shared fixture (grounded — head — X — head — Y, both stranded), with fixture preconditions asking the solver directly so a fixture that stops producing a knot says so rather than silently retargeting the row. The mutation that matters is one-directional (`Stranded` returning `"grounded"`), because swapping the two arms also breaks pre-existing rows and is therefore not the sharp test: a brick the solver **gave up on** reading to a human as *resting on the earth* is the same fail-open direction `Integration.PullingSupportBringsTheWallDown` exists to police.

  **Two gaps remain deliberately open** *(recorded 2026-08-05)*:

  - **A joint with `BreakPass >= 1` reads "broken (went with a removed piece)", which becomes a LIE the day the cascade reaches the world wire** (rank 1). Unreachable today only because `FStructureBinding` exposes no `SolveAndBreak` and `GetStructure()` is const-only, so there is no seam a red test could use without adding untested production API to make one. **Write its failing test at the same time as that item, not after.** Zero-cost alternative if it should not wait: drop the parenthetical to a bare `"broken"`, which is unconditionally true — a test edit, since the wording is pinned.
  - **`EJointRole::None → "no tier"` is unreachable and uncovered.** `AddConnection` refuses a normal that will not normalise and `InspectPiece` emits rows only for real joints on real pieces, so it is a fail-closed default arm, the same category as a post-switch `return`.

  **THE PANEL IS DRAWN AND WIRED, AND SIX THINGS ABOUT IT ARE UNOBSERVED** *(2026-08-05)*. `BuildPieceMenuWidget` draws `CountText`, an `SButton` per selected brick showing its `Label`, and the singled-out brick's `SupportText`, `JointsText` and one line per joint; `OnHovered` calls `SetInspectedPiece`, `OnUnhovered` clears it. `EBrickHighlight::Inspected` is a fourth state with its own overlay (`M_BrickInspected`, magenta at 0.85, stronger than selected's 0.6), and `HighlightForPiece` returns it **first**, gated on the ref also being in the selection — which is what makes a stale inspected ref inert instead of needing clearing at three call sites.

  **The readout lives in its own `SBox` whose CONTENT is swapped, and that is forced rather than stylistic.** Rebuilding the whole panel on hover destroys the very button the cursor is on, so Slate fires `OnHovered` on its replacement next frame, and again on the one after — a rebuild every frame. The box holds nothing interactive, so it cannot feed itself, and there is still exactly one `AddViewportWidgetContent`/`RemoveViewportWidgetContent` pair. Dead entries are styled from `bIsLivePiece` by a colour ternary, deliberately **not** `IsEnabled`, because a disabled `SButton` fires no hover events at all.

  **Unobserved, in the order that would bite** — nobody has pressed Play, and this project has twice shipped a green suite over something invisible:
  - **The magenta material's SHADER COMPILATION is as unproven as the other two.** A material whose shader failed to compile has exactly the right node graph and renders as the default checkerboard. This is the biggest remaining hole of the Nanite shape.
  - **The hover/rebuild re-entrancy argument is reasoning, not observation.** Nobody has moved a mouse over an entry.
  - **Whether `SButton` hover routes at all on the opening frame**, which depends on the viewport releasing mouse capture — the recorded capture argument says it does so on the LMB-up that opens the menu.
  - **Whether a click on an entry row is swallowed.** `OnClicked` is unbound; `SButton::OnMouseButtonDown` is believed to still return `Handled`, so a click does nothing rather than falling through to the wall behind. Unverified.
  - **Whether the panel is legible.** Engine-default `STextBlock`s in a centred `SBox`, no sizing, no scroll, no background. Three bricks with three joints each is ~10 lines; **a large selection will run off the screen.**
  - **Whether magenta reads as stronger than cyan on a brick at playing distance.** Argued at the display, not judged by eye.

  **Four costs recorded rather than paid** *(2026-08-05)*: `bIsLivePiece` costs a full `InspectPiece` per entry, which scans every connection to read one bool — O(selection × connections) per readout build, on every hover enter *and* leave; sub-millisecond today, and the honest fix if it ever matters is a cheaper liveness accessor on the model, never a cache in the widget. `PieceMenuInspectorForSelection()` is called twice per build. `SetInspectedPiece` is not idempotent-guarded (harmless with the content-swap; it would not have been with a full rebuild). And **`PieceMenuInspectorForSelection()` is private, so no test can assert the controller feeds the widget the right inspector** — making it public is the cheap slice that would shrink the untested inch to "Slate draws it", and it is the highest-value small test left in this area.

  **Three shape notes from before the widget landed; the first two are now answered** *(from review, 2026-08-05)*:

  - **The entry list carries no liveness.** `FInspectorPieceEntry` is `{ Ref, Label, bIsInspected }`, so a removed brick, a ref naming another structure and a live brick present identically. A widget wanting to grey out the dead one would have to resolve the ref itself — logic straight back out of the model, at exactly the surface the **rank-0 product decision** is meant to become legible on. `BuildPieceMenuInspector` calls `InspectPiece` only for the inspected ref, so it does not know either. **Decide this with the widget, not after it.**
  - **A live brick with no joints has no sentence.** `bHasInspectedPiece` is true and `Joints` is empty (the floater case, covered), and there is no model-side string for it — so a widget wanting "no joints" must branch on `Joints.Num() == 0`. `CountText`'s "No bricks selected" sets the precedent that an empty gets its own sentence; this one does not have one yet.
  - **`ForceN` is `Size()`, so the SIGN is discarded.** Intentional, and `Role` recovers bed-above from bed-below — but not for a head joint, and the sign of `GetConnectionForce` is what distinguishes a joint being *pulled* from one being *pushed*. A destruction game whose readout cannot show tension is worth settling before the shape sets.

  Note it interacts with the product decision two entries down: a per-brick readout is exactly the surface on which "picked but no longer actionable" would become legible.

- **A CASCADE THAT RELEASES A *SELECTED* BRICK BREAKS AN INVARIANT THIS PROJECT ALREADY ASSERTS ELSEWHERE, AND WHICH WAY TO RESOLVE IT IS A PRODUCT DECISION, NOT A BUG FIX** *(recorded 2026-08-04, deliberately not chosen)*. `World.Select`'s `CheckMultiSelectMenu` pins **a menu is up exactly when the selection is non-empty**. A released piece still satisfies `PieceActionsFor`'s *resolve*, but fails `Delete`'s `CanRun` (`!IsPieceRemoved && !IsReleased`), so the **intersection empties**, `BuildPieceMenuRows` builds nothing, and `ShowPieceMenu(empty)` takes the menu down — while `PieceSelection` stays non-empty and `HighlightForPiece` reads **the selection, not the menu**. The state that leaves a player in is **bricks still glowing `Selected`, no menu, and no way to clear them except clicking empty space**, which is the worst of the two options rather than a chosen one.

  **Both readings are defensible and the choice is about what a selection MEANS**, so it is left open rather than settled by whoever next touches the code:

  - *A selection is what you have picked.* Keep the bricks lit; the menu is legitimately empty because there is nothing that can be done to falling debris. Then the fix is to make the state escapable and legible — some route back to an empty selection that is not "click the sky", and possibly a third highlight for "picked but no longer actionable".
  - *A selection is what the menu is about.* Then a piece leaving the actionable set must leave the selection, the highlight goes with it, and the invariant holds unchanged. Cheaper, and it silently discards a pick the player made.

  **Latent today**, and only because nothing in `World/` calls `SolveAndBreak` (see the world-binding section) — so the only way a selected piece gets released is a cascade that does not yet run in a game. It becomes reachable the day that wire lands, and it should be decided **before** it does, not discovered afterwards.

- **`OnHoverPiece` RUNS AN `ECC_Visibility` LINE TRACE ON EVERY FRAME THE MOUSE MOVES, AND NOBODY HAS MEASURED IT** *(left deliberately 2026-08-04)*. That is the direct cost of binding on `Triggered`, which is the right trigger (see `SetupInputComponent`) — a mouse axis actuates on exactly the frames the mouse moved, which are exactly the frames on which what is under the cursor can have changed, and a still mouse costs nothing at all. Against the game mode's 1,220-brick wall that is one trace per moved frame. **Do not pre-optimise it**: a trace against a static scene is what the collision acceleration structure is for, and the obvious "optimisations" (throttling to every Nth frame, or only re-tracing when the cursor moved more than N pixels) both reintroduce the staleness `Triggered` was chosen to avoid. If it ever does show up in a profile, the honest fix is a cheaper trace, not a lazier one. Measuring needs a viewport and a real player, so it belongs to the same session that judges whether the overlays look right.

- **THE WIDGET WAS WRITTEN WITHOUT A FAILING TEST, UNDER AN EXPLICIT EXCEPTION RECORDED HERE** *(2026-08-04)*. It is **not** one of CLAUDE.md's two standing exemptions (build configuration, and content/`.ini`) — it is C++ in the game module, and it was landed anyway, deliberately and on the record.

  **Why.** A code-built test world has no `UGameViewportClient` at all, so `AddViewportWidgetContent` has nothing to add to and no headless assertion can see a button, a click, or a leak. The gate would therefore refuse this inch **forever**, and the MVP would never close. The whole slice sequence before it exists to make the inch as small as it can possibly be: everything the menu could get wrong — which rows, which ref, which action, the dismissal, the controls restore, an index naming no row — is decided by `ChoosePieceMenuRow` and the presenter, and every one of those is covered. What is genuinely untestable is only *a button exists per row, and clicking it calls `ChoosePieceMenuRow(MyIndex)`*.

  **So the rule that replaces the test is: PUT NO LOGIC IN THE WIDGET.** Anything there is untested by construction. Today it is one `SButton` per `GetShownPieceMenuRows()` entry, the label taken from the row, `OnClicked` → `ChoosePieceMenuRow(index)`, built beside the `Append` in `ShowPieceMenu` and removed beside the `Reset` in `DismissPieceMenu` so it inherits the dismiss-then-build discipline. **A branch, a filter, a `CanRun` re-check or a remembered ref appearing in `BuildPieceMenuWidget` is the drift to watch for** — each would be a second, quieter copy of a policy that already has one covered home. If the widget ever needs to *decide* something, the decision belongs behind `ChoosePieceMenuRow` or in `Core/`, where a test can reach it.

- **SOMEBODY HAS NOW PRESSED PLAY, AND THE FIRST OF THESE TWO IS ANSWERED** *(2026-08-04)*. A player clicked a brick, got the menu, chose Delete and the brick went — so the deprojection, the widget and the click reaching the button all work in a running game, and the mouse-capture reasoning below is confirmed by a click rather than only by reading engine source. **What the same session found is the commit-path push bug** (world-binding section): the brick went and the wall did not come down. **Keep the capture paragraph** — it is the record of *why* it works, and the fallback if it ever stops. The second unknown, whether the menu looks reasonable where it is drawn, is still open and is cosmetic.

  - **Whether a click reaches the button at all, which is a mouse-CAPTURE question rather than a Slate one — ANSWERED YES BY A REAL CLICK, reasoning kept.** The project never calls `SetInputMode`, so the viewport uses the `UInputSettings` default (`CapturePermanently_IncludingInitialMouseDown`), and while the viewport holds capture Slate routes every mouse event to it rather than hit-testing the widgets above it. **Read from engine source, it works out**: `FSceneViewport::OnMouseButtonUp` (`Runtime/Engine/Private/Slate/SceneViewport.cpp`, ~line 826) releases capture whenever `ViewportClient->GetCursor(...) != EMouseCursor::None`, and `UGameViewportClient::GetCursor` defers to `APlayerController::GetMouseCursor`, which is non-`None` exactly when `bShowMouseCursor` is set — which `SetPieceMenuControls(true)` did on the press that opened the menu. So the LMB-**up** that follows the opening click releases capture, and the next click lands on the button. **This was reasoned, not observed.** If it turns out to be wrong the fix is `SetInputMode(FInputModeGameAndUI())` in `SetPieceMenuControls`, and it must go in that one function with its restore, never beside it. One known ragged edge either way: if a click were released before the tick that processes the press, capture would still be held and the *first* click on the menu would go to the viewport instead — which dismisses and re-opens, so it self-heals in one click and is not worth guarding for speculatively.
  - **Whether the menu is usable where it is drawn.** It is a centred `SBox` of stacked buttons, i.e. in the middle of the screen rather than at the cursor, and it is styled with engine defaults. Nothing depends on either, and both are cosmetic — but nobody has seen it, so "it looks reasonable" is unverified too. Clicking away from the buttons should dismiss (the `SBox` and the panel are self-hit-test-invisible, so the click reaches the viewport and `IA_InspectPiece` misses, which dismisses).

- **NO TEST IN THIS PROJECT HAS EVER RENDERED A PIXEL, AND THE NANITE BUG IS WHAT THAT COSTS** *(recorded 2026-08-04)*. Every suite run is `-nullrhi`, so the whole renderer is absent. The highlight is now covered at three levels — the controller decides the right state, the brick attaches the right material, and the material folds to a visible colour — and **all three were green while the player saw nothing**, because the layer underneath them was never exercised. That is the same shape as the missing-world-push bug one storey down: correct parts, and the join untested.

  **`BrickMeshOverlayTest` closes this particular hole by asserting the precondition rather than the pixel**, which is the right trade and is as far as a `-nullrhi` process can go. **What is still uncovered, in the order it is worth closing:**

  - **That the overlay SHADERS COMPILED.** Both materials were authored headlessly by Python plus `recompile_material`, and the paint test reads the **node graph** — a material whose shader failed to compile has exactly that graph and renders as the default checkerboard. Same family as the Nanite bug: correct data, silent renderer substitution, green suite. This is the biggest remaining hole of the same shape.
  - **That the renderer actually takes the `FStaticMeshSceneProxy` path.** `UseNanite(ShaderPlatform)` is the other conjunct of `ShouldCreateNaniteProxy` and is deliberately *not* asserted, because it is a property of the machine rather than of the asset and would make the suite green on every headless box and red only where there is a GPU.
  - **That the overlay composites legibly over the brick's real base material under the sandbox's lighting and exposure.** The 0.18 grey stand-in is an arbitrary proxy for it.

  **The recommended real-RHI test is NOT a screenshot, and this is the useful conclusion.** Cheapest first: assert **`Nanite::FNaniteResourcesHelper::ShouldCreateNaniteProxy(*Component, nullptr) == false`** on a *registered, in-world* brick. It is the engine's own decision function, it evaluates **both** conjuncts, `BrickWorldTestSupport` already builds the world it needs, and it returns a **boolean** — no tolerance, no baseline, no driver variance. It cannot run in the standard `-nullrhi` suite, so it needs its own documented command, and that is its whole cost.

  A screenshot buys exactly one thing that does not: proof the shader compiled and the batch reached the screen. **If it is ever written it must be a DIFFERENCE test** — one camera, one brick, three captures (`None` / `Hovered` / `Selected`), asserting the brick's pixels differ pairwise — and **never** an absolute RGB comparison, which is hostage to tone mapping, auto-exposure warm-up and driver differences and will flake. It also needs a map under `Content/Maps/FunctionalTests/` and the gated `FunctionalTesting` dependency per CLAUDE.md. **Not worth it yet**: its marginal value over the proxy assertion is better bought by a shader-compilation check plus one human pressing Play.

- **`World.Scenario.GameModeBuildsTheWallOnBeginPlay` asserts the MODEL, not the outcome, and has the same gap the integration set was written to close** *(recorded 2026-08-04, deliberately not fixed)*. It checks that the game mode built and solved the wall by reading `GetPieceSupport` — which is exactly the mechanism-not-outcome reading that stayed green while a real wall stood still. It is the **fourth** integration test the entry rule implies and it was never written: enter through `BeginPlay` as a player's session does, tick, and assert the wall is physically standing where it was built rather than that its handles report `Grounded`. Lower value than the three that exist, because the failure it would catch is "the scenario never reaches the world at all", which is loud — but it is the one remaining place where the suite still takes a model reading as proof of a physical fact.

- **`ChoosePieceMenuRow` dereferences the chosen row's `Action` without a null check, and nothing guards it** *(left 2026-08-04.)* `BuildPieceMenuRows` skips a null-action row, so no production route can present one, and `ShowPieceMenu` takes a `TArrayView` from anywhere — so a hand-built malformed row would crash the process at the commit rather than failing anything. It was left out because it is branching logic with no failing test behind it, and because a crash is the *loud* direction: the failure mode this project actually pays for is the silent wrong answer. Closing it is one red row in `World.Choose` (present a row with a null `Action`, choose it, expect a refusal and nothing entered) and then the guard. Same family as the aliasing entry below.

## The graph representation — three assumptions that will break later

The structure is a graph: pieces are nodes, connections are edges, stored as a flat **edge list** (`Pieces` and `Connections` in `Structure.h`), with `PieceA`/`PieceB` as `int32` positions in the piece array. Adjacency is not stored — every `SolveLoads` derives three purpose-built adjacency structures over the **support relation** (`SupportConnections`, `Loaders`, `LoadPaths`), which is correct, because who supports whom changes as joints break and so cannot be cached on the pieces.

Three things are true today, load-bearing, and written down nowhere else. None blocks current work; each is cheap now and expensive later. Ordered by that ratio.

> **Handle stability is no longer one of them** *(closed 2026-08-01)*. `RemovePiece` **tombstones** the slot: the piece is marked dead, the hole stays, and no piece ever moves down into it, so every handle means the same piece for the lifetime of the structure. `NumPieces` deliberately still returns the handle **range** and `NumLivePieces` is the separate count — a `NumPieces` that shrank would silently skip real pieces in every `0..NumPieces()` loop. The reasoning against "tidying" the tombstone into a **free list** now lives in the code, on `FStructurePiece::bIsInTheStructure`, which is where it can actually stop the change: reusing a slot without a generation counter makes a stale handle name a *different live piece*, which nothing can detect. Reuse needs **generational handles**, not a free list. Driven by `Tests/StructureRemovalTest.cpp`; the model-level statement is DESIGN.md §3, "Removing a piece takes it out of the graph".

- **The solver is global, but collapse is local.** One `SolveLoads` walks every piece. Break a wall in half and the two independent structures stay in one array and are re-solved together — and `SolveAndBreak` now does that once per breaking pass, so knocking one brick out of a building re-solves the whole building, repeatedly. The answer is connected-component islands solved independently plus dirty-region tracking, so only the component that changed re-solves. The current shape neither resists nor helps this. Expect it to be the first of the four to actually hurt.
- **Pieces have no position, and force delivery is inherently spatial.** "A piece is a mass and an identity" is why the maths is fast, deterministic and testable without a world, and it should stay that way. But it means the solver can never answer *which pieces are near this point* — and explosions with radial falloff and kinetic impacts, both open threads in DESIGN.md §6, start exactly there. Something above the solver must own geometry and hand down piece handles. **`Core/Layout` is now the first instance of that boundary and settles its shape** — geometry above, handles down, a one-way include, and `FBrickLayout` holding boxes parallel to the piece array — so the question is no longer *where* the seam goes. What is still undesigned is the **query** side: `FBrickLayout` is a flat array with no spatial index, so "which pieces are near this point" is still a linear scan of somebody else's array, and nothing keeps a box in step with a piece that has been removed or released to dynamics. That is the conversation force delivery actually needs.
- **Connections are only ever destroyed, never created at runtime.** Breaking is monotonic. Real collapse does the opposite too — debris piles up and a fallen slab comes to rest on something and starts carrying load. Under this model rubble can never support anything. Fine for the MVP and for a good while after, but it needs contact detection, so it lands on top of the item above.

One smaller live footgun: connections are stored undirected, but **the force sign depends on which end is `PieceA`** (`Structure.cpp`'s accumulation, and `ConnectionLoad.h`'s convention that the force belongs to `PieceB`). Anything that ever rebuilds, sorts or deduplicates the connection array must preserve that orientation, or compression silently becomes tension.

## Core systems — designed, not built

DESIGN.md §2–3 specifies all of it.

**`FConnectionStrength` is a plain C++ struct, not a data asset.** DESIGN.md §2 calls for a data asset and for materials to be "data, not code". Half of that is now true — `Core/Profiles` makes adding a material or a connection type **adding a row**, with no new class and no new branch — but it is still a row in a `.cpp`, so it needs a recompile and there is no `USTRUCT`, no `UPROPERTY`, nothing tunable in the editor. Correctly deferred rather than speculatively built; this is the remaining gap between the current shape and the stated design.

**Shear direction is discarded.** `FConnectionLoad::Shear` is a magnitude only. Fine for isotropic materials, but DESIGN.md notes wood fails by splintering *along the grain* — grain-relative shear direction will matter once anisotropic materials arrive. Revisit then; don't add it speculatively.

**Base destructible actor** wrapping a Chaos Geometry Collection, with damage threshold and connection strength, exposing apply-damage-at-location. Needs a live world and a ticking solver, so expect integration-shaped tests rather than unit tests.

**Material profile data asset** — density and directional strengths now exist in `Core/Profiles/MaterialProfiles`; **fracture pattern** does not, and neither does anything authorable in the editor (the data-asset gap noted above).

**Connection types as data profiles** — mortar, nail, screw, bolt all exist in `Core/Profiles/ConnectionProfiles`, and `Core/Connection` takes an `FConnectionStrength` whole. What is missing is the **in-game builder choosing between them**, and the data-asset gap noted above.

**A second, dissimilar material — wood — added exactly once.** DESIGN.md §4: don't write a test per material; add wood one time only, to prove the directional code genuinely reads the profile rather than having brick's numbers baked in, by running the same shear scenario on it and confirming it survives where brick failed. **Deliberately not added with the profile library** — adding it alongside the calibration data would spend that proof for nothing. Goes together with the connection-to-material pairing rule that `BondFactor` is reserved for.

**Damage / force manager** — routes hits, explosions, radial forces to the right actors.

**Piece-size floor + three modes.** Build modes 1 (indestructible) and 3 (disappear) first — they prove threshold detection simply. Mode 2 (Niagara dust) after.

**Piece creation timestamps** — stamped when a piece breaks free, inspectable live, used to recover failure sequence.

**Scenario base class + tiny default scenario.** DESIGN.md §5: the scaffold spawns a *scenario* and knows nothing about bricks. Brick wall is one scenario. Also needs the in-world overlay menu (scenario switcher + strain readouts) — the game should never be an empty void.

## Open design threads (DESIGN.md §6 — not yet designed)

**Force delivery systems** — explosions with radial falloff, kinetic impacts from large objects. DESIGN.md suggests this as the natural next design topic: everything so far is about *receiving* force, nothing *delivers* it.

**Decided 2026-08-01: sideways force delivery is NOT in the MVP, and the reason is structural rather than a priority call.** Do not read "no explosions planned" as scope-trimming that could be reversed cheaply — there is a dependency underneath it.

The MVP makes a wall come down two ways, both of which work on the code that exists today:

- **(A) Removal.** Knock a piece out; load re-routes, pieces strand, the cascade runs. **The solver half is done**: `FStructure::RemovePiece` tombstones the slot and severs the joints that held the piece, and `Tests/StructureRemovalTest.cpp` covers redistribution onto the survivors, promotion of a head joint when the bed joint beneath goes, a removal that cascades, and the degenerate handles. What is left is entirely above the solver — something that lets a player *name* a piece to remove, which is the brick actor and the scenario.
- **(B) Downward external load.** A heavy piece resting on top, a roof load. Needs *nothing new*: a downward load is gravity-aligned, so the existing routing handles it unchanged, and it is just a piece with a large mass. Lime mortar gives at roughly 18 t. Probably the cheapest possible route to a visible collapse.

**(C) Arbitrary-direction force is deferred** because it is not additive. A joint is a bed joint when its normal is within 45° of vertical, and a piece's supports are the bed joints *beneath* it — gravity is wired into the routing itself, not just into the loads. An explosion pushing a wall sideways makes "beneath" meaningless, so C requires generalising the support relation to the direction of the applied load. That is the same limitation already recorded against the bed/head tier, and it is a rewrite of the most load-bearing assumption in the solver rather than a feature bolted on top.

**C, THE ECCENTRIC-LOAD / BENDING-MOMENT ITEM AND SECONDARY DEBRIS DAMAGE ARE ONE DEPENDENCY WEARING THREE HATS, AND THE SPATIAL LAYER IS NOW THE NAMED NEXT DESIGN SLICE, ASKED FOR EXPLICITLY** *(2026-08-04)*. All three need pieces to have positions and joints to have extent, so designing the spatial layer once for all of them is far cheaper than three times — whoever picks up any one of them should look at the other two first. The full design-level treatment is in **DESIGN.md §6, "The spatial layer"**; what follows is the state of it.

**The third hat was reported from a running game** as "bricks falling don't do much damage to those beneath". That is **expected today and is not a bug**: Chaos resolves the collision physically, so debris bounces and piles correctly, but nothing tells the *solver* it happened — breakage is decided from static load alone, so a brick can be struck at speed by a falling course and its joints never hear about it.

All three want the same missing thing, and it is worth stating as one sentence because it is the reason none of them is cheap on its own: **`FStructure` deliberately has no positions.** That is what keeps the solver world-free, keeps the suite under a second and lets the fuzzes run twelve thousand cases — so this is a design slice against a deliberate decision, not an oversight to patch. What each hat needs from it:

- **Moments** — where a load is applied relative to the joint's centroid, plus the joint's extent and axis (a section modulus), so an edge stress can be computed instead of an average. Detail under the load solver.
- **Arbitrary-direction force (C)** — the support relation generalised to the direction of the applied load, since "beneath" is meaningless once a wall is pushed sideways.
- **Debris damage** — an impact point and an impulse arriving from the world into the graph, which is the first force in the project that is neither gravity nor a static external load.

**The honest sequencing is design-then-build, and the design comes first for all three at once.** Doing moments alone would fix the visible artefact — the standing cantilevers a player can already see, where head-joint fallback holds a hanging brick at ~2% of shear capacity and roughly fifty in a chain would be needed to fail in shear, while the *real* failure is the joint peeling open in tension at a hundredth the strength. But it would fix it by adding half a spatial layer, and the other two would then either re-litigate it or grow a second one beside it.

Why this matters for the MVP at all: a four-course brick wall sits at about **0.0004 utilisation** under its own weight. Masonry is enormously overbuilt in compression, so gravity alone will *never* break a wall — without A or B, phases 3–6 produce a wall that stands there looking correct and does nothing.

**Visual break patterns** per material — wood splintering, concrete fracturing, glass shattering. Distinct from *when* things collapse.

**Secondary debris collisions** — debris carrying momentum into other pieces and knocking more loose. **Observed in a running game as absent** *(2026-08-04)* and now folded into the spatial-layer slice above as its third hat; it is not a separate thread any more.

**Performance at full-building scale** — individually-massed pieces plus live debris gets heavy fast. Flagged as a known risk, no plan yet.
