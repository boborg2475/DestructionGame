What holds a corbel up, and how far a cut peels back

Four acceptance rows are red and they reduce to these two questions. Neither is a bug to fix — both are choices about what the game should say, and each has a cost I can put a number on. Everything below is measured, not estimated.

Corrected — I gave two wrong answers before this page was checked

I suggested that case 10 fails because its section is over-credited, and that case 20's teeth should hold. Checked against the printed fixture output, both are wrong.

The 0.293 / 60 cm I quoted belongs to the staircase's course-5 rung, not case 10 — whose worst joint reads 0.299975. And case 10's survivor is a filled wedge, not a staircase of single bricks: every course runs continuously back to the wall's face, so a section-plane rule moves it not one digit. Decision 1 stands exactly as written below.

And case 20's teeth are not one-brick chains — they carry 29 and 12.5 brick weights, sitting at the bottom of a load funnel. At 0.41573 per brick weight that is 12× and 5× over capacity. They must break, and nine fall is the right answer — the recommendation below is unchanged, but for the opposite reason to the one I gave you verbally.

Decision 1 · cases 10, 19 and 12

A wall standing on ninety centimetres of nothing

Three rows fail the same way. After the cut, what survives is a half-brick-per-course corbel front projecting into thin air off a free end — and the model is perfectly happy with it.

No joint check can catch this. The corbel ladder does not cross capacity until about 36 steps, so any threshold set low enough to condemn these three also condemns the corbels you have already ruled must stand. That is not a tuning problem — it is a missing kind of check. This model has only ever asked questions about joints, never about whole assemblies.

Build the overturning checkrecommended

A new class of check — whole-assembly stability about a pivot — and it would be the first thing in this model that is not a question about a single joint. The rule is the table above: gather the assembly, find the pivot, and ask whether restoring mass beyond it reaches the ground independently.

It also recovers something you knowingly gave up. When you ruled on case 14, the cost recorded was that the model could no longer express a corbel failing by projecting too far. This is that capability, arriving by the correct mechanism rather than by a threshold.

Decision 2 · case 20

The staircase face peels, and it cannot stop at two

You ruled that exactly two loose teeth drop and the mass stands. Tracing what removing those two does to the course above shows that set is not reachable by any consistent rule.

The two teeth are not distinguishable from the seven above them. c3/4.5 has an intact head joint to a half-seated neighbour that reaches the ground — which is exactly the relationship c4/4 has to its neighbour. Any rule that saves the seven saves the two; any rule that drops the two drops the seven.

Where this leaves the suite

138 tests, 4 failures. Decision 2 clears one row for free. Decision 1 clears three, but only by building something.

And the fourth red may be the one that matters most. Chasing the two corrections above led back to it: the counterweight buying nothing, case B (filled) reading worse than case A (bare), and a single tooth in case 20 carrying twenty-nine brick weights are one defect wearing three faces — load routes only downward, so it funnels onto whatever corbelled column sits beneath it while masonry beside or behind the failure contributes nothing at all.

That is the same gap as the free-end cascade and the jamb reveal, it already has a failing test (CorbelStepsBeforeTensionWins, written down as an expected failure before it was run), and it plausibly moves cases 10, 12, 19 and 20 together — which is what both of my corrected claims were trying to do the hard way. If one thing gets built next, I would argue for that rather than either decision on this page.

Nothing here is urgent and nothing is committed. Both rows can also be left red with the reasoning recorded, which is honest as long as the catalogue says so — a test softened until it passes is worse than one that is openly red.
