Where the counterweight runs out

E and F as you asked, plus four more the work so far says are missing. E turned out to be sharper than a step count — working out what to expect exposed a prediction that would be a real defect, and it is cheap to check.

The prediction E exists to test

I think our model reads C and D identically — that the counterweight does nothing at all. The masonry behind the corbel carries its own weight down its own columns to the ground; none of it passes through the corbel’s root joint. So the axial compression at that joint is unchanged, the section is unchanged, and the reading should be unchanged.

If that is right, the crossover step count for D is the same as for C, and “more bricks opposite” buys nothing in the current model. That would be the third face of the same downward-only routing defect — after the free end and the jamb reveal. E finds out in one run.

The two you asked for

Both built on case D’s geometry: a filled stepped corbel off an immovable base, with three cells of masonry opposite.

Four more the work says are missing

You asked for scale, materials and material types to work. Nothing currently tests any of the three, and the design makes specific claims about all of them.

Why these six

E and F pin the ends of the same curve. E finds where it crosses; F guarantees it does cross, so no future change can quietly make an absurd overhang stand. Together they turn “something must still break” from an argument into two assertions.

G, H and I are the three things you asked for that nothing checks. The design claims the rule is covariant under scale, that dry stone gets almost no composite depth, and that a different bond changes the answer automatically because the lever arm is measured rather than assumed. All three are stated as theorems and none has a fixture. H needs a new profile row — data, not code — because dry stone’s tensile strength is an exact zero, which condemns it at any section and so hides whether the depth rule did anything.

J is the number that decides λ, and the design says so in as many words: the window looked wider than it is because no fixture presents a free-end deletion in a wall much taller than the cut — which is the wall the game renders. Without J the constant gets picked against fixtures that cannot see the case it matters for.

Say go and I will write them as red tests, or tell me which to drop. None of the expected numbers here are measured — they are worked forward from the identities in the design, and the design has been wrong seven times, so the tests will derive their own and report where they disagree.
