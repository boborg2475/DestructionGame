Above, opposite, and what actually holds a corbel up

Four stepped walls off a grounded column, varying one thing at a time. They exist to test the mechanism you described — “a larger overhang requires more bricks on top of and opposite of the overhang” — and one of them shows that “on top” and “opposite” are not the same mechanism, and can point in opposite directions.

The base is immovable, and that simplifies everything

Because the base cannot translate, none of these can topple as a rigid body — a corbel rotating about its bearing edge is the bed joint at that level opening in tension across its back. Overturning and joint failure are the same event here. So there is no second mechanism to build: our model already computes that joint, and what it may get wrong is the section it credits to it.

The moments shown are about that root joint’s outer edge — what pulls it open against what holds it shut, in brick‑weight‑centimetres. A brick is 21.5 × 10.25 × 6.5 cm at 1.9 g/cm³, so 26.69 N; each step out is 11.25 cm, half a cell. Blue bricks are the immovable base; grey is everything the root joint has to hold.

What these four are actually asking

A against B is the real question, and it is the one your correction sharpened. A is a stair of single bricks with nothing to act with; B is the same profile filled in, which is the shape your screenshot actually had. If filling makes the difference, composite action is doing something real. If A and B read the same, the model is crediting depth that is not there — and A is the proof, because A has almost no masonry above the root and should not be rescued by any honest rule.

C is the one the depth bound has to get right. Reach ten steps instead of four and the moment about the root climbs with the square of the reach while the base is unchanged. If C still stands, there is no limit on what bonding can rescue — the concern that started this thread.

D tests whether “opposite” needs any new code at all. Masonry behind the root puts weight on the back of the joint, and closing a joint under compression is already what the axial term does — it is what separated cases 15 and 16 by a factor of 32. If C fails and D stands, the counterweight is being read correctly and nothing new is required. If D also fails, weight behind the joint is not reaching it.

Only case A’s reading is measured — it maps onto the corbel you already ruled on. The other three are marked unmeasured rather than quoted, because this design has been wrong seven times and a test caught every one.
